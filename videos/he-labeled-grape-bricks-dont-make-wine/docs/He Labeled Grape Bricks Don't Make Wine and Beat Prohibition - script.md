## Script (v2, v10.9 hook formula)
A guy beat Prohibition by selling grape bricks with a warning not to make wine.
You see, America just banned alcohol, and his grapes are wine grapes.
His neighbors rip out their vines. One laughs, "Nobody's buying a grape brick, Brick Boy."
He just smiles. "Read the label."
Every brick gets a warning: do not dissolve this in a gallon of water, and do not leave the jug in a cupboard for twenty-one days.
The whole town laughs. Who puts a warning on juice?
Then federal agents pull up. The neighbors line the fence to watch Brick Boy get arrested.
An agent picks up a brick, reads the warning out loud... and puts it back. Because Washington just ruled it legal.
Bricks fly off the shelves. They even send a guy to your house to set up the jug, then come back and bottle it.
That's the trick. The law banned selling wine, but every family could make two hundred gallons of fruit juice at home a year. And if your juice happens to sit in a cupboard for twenty-one days, well, that's your juice.
It's like selling cake mix with a warning: do not bake at three-fifty.
California's vineyards almost double. The neighbors are planting their vines back.
And the government's top Prohibition lawyer quits... to become the grape farmers' lawyer.
So was Brick Boy a criminal, or just the only guy who read the law?
Twenty-one days later, the whole country is drinking juice. And it all started...

## Facts (verified)
- **The law:** the Volstead Act (in force January 1920) banned making and selling intoxicating drinks. Section 29 let a household make "non-intoxicating" fruit juice at home, up to 200 gallons a year. [Wine History Project](https://winehistoryproject.org/wine-grapes-during-prohibition/), [Wikipedia: Vine-Glo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vine-Glo)
- **The bricks:**
  - Grape bricks were concentrate blocks, about the size of a pound of butter and about $1 each, dissolved in a gallon of water. [Wine History Project](https://winehistoryproject.org/wine-grapes-during-prohibition/)
  - The label warned not to leave the jug in the cupboard for about 21 days, or it would turn into wine. [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/ingenious-brick-helped-wine-industry-survive-prohibition-180956412/), [Grape Collective](https://grapecollective.com/articles/prohibitions-grape-bricks-how-to-not-make-wine)
- **The house calls:** a serviceman came to the house, set it up, then came back to rack and bottle it (Vine-Glo service, Philo Biane interview). [Wine History Project](https://winehistoryproject.org/prohibition-fruit-industries-ltd/)
- **The vineyards:** California had about 300,000 acres of vines in 1919, and that almost doubled by 1926. [Wine History Project](https://winehistoryproject.org/wine-grapes-during-prohibition/)
- **"Washington ruled it legal":** Assistant Attorney General Mabel Walker Willebrandt ruled Vine-Glo legal, and Prohibition agents were told not to interfere. After leaving government she became Fruit Industries' attorney. [Wikipedia: Vine-Glo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vine-Glo), [Wine History Project](https://winehistoryproject.org/prohibition-fruit-industries-ltd/)
- **The farmer:** "Brick Boy" is an unnamed composite farmer, as in our other videos. The bricks really were sold by California grape growers and their co-op, Fruit Industries.
- **Left out:** "wine drinking doubled" and the exact grape prices each had only one source, so neither is in the script.
