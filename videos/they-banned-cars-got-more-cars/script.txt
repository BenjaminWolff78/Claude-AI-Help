A city banned every car one day a week, and ended up with way more cars than before.
You see, in 1989 Mexico City's smog is so bad the mayor bans every car one weekday, by its plate number.
Memo's plate ends in five, so on Mondays his neighbor Hugo drives by laughing, "Enjoy the bus, pal!"
But 2.3 million drivers share that problem.
So Memo comes home with a second car, a rusty one whose plate ends in eight.
Hugo howls, "Nice barbecue, Mister Two Cars!"
Memo just says, "Check the plate."
At first the ban looks perfect.
Police stop 8,000 cheaters on day one, and the mayor brags about clean air.
Wait, how does buying more cars beat a car ban?
By Friday three neighbors own a wreck too, and by Christmas the whole street drives junk that lost a race to its own smoke.
That's the trick: the rule bans a plate, not a driver.
Two plates mean you drive all week.
It's like banning your kid's sneakers on Mondays, and he just stomps out in his old boots.
Economists count 325,000 extra cars, and the air never gets any cleaner.
So was Mister Two Cars a cheater, or the only one doing the math?
In 2008 the city fixed it the only way it knew how.
It banned cars on Saturdays too.
