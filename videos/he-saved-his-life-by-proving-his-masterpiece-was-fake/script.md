## Script (counts below)
A painter saved his own life by proving his masterpiece was a fake.
You see, it's 1945, and a three-hundred-year-old masterpiece just turned up in a top Nazi's stash. Guess who sold it?
Selling a national treasure to the enemy is treason. And treason means death.
So he goes, "Relax. It's not old. I painted it."
The guards laugh. The judge laughs. The newspapers call it the dumbest excuse of the war.
But how does one guy fool every expert in Europe?
Years earlier, critics trashed his paintings in one line: "Every virtue except originality."
So he stopped being original.
He paints a "lost" old masterpiece, and the country's top expert calls it the masterpiece of the century.
A museum pays a fortune. He paints another. And another.
Then a top Nazi trades a hundred and thirty-seven real paintings for one of them.
So he goes, "Fine. Give me paint, a canvas... and an oven."
That's the trick. Old paint is rock hard, so he mixes plastic into his, and bakes the canvas like cookies.
Rolls it over a pipe till it cracks, rubs ink in the cracks. Three hundred years, in one afternoon.
The experts test his fakes, and inside the paint they find plastic. Plastic that wasn't invented until nineteen-oh-seven.
The traitor becomes the guy who scammed a Nazi, and the country ranks him its second favorite man.
So, con man or war hero?
Every virtue except originality. And that's what saved his life.

## Facts (checked)
- He was a Dutch painter whose 1920s critics said his work copied the Old Masters too closely. One wrote that he "has every virtue except originality".
- He bought real 17th-century canvases, scraped them, and hardened his paint with phenol-formaldehyde (Bakelite). He baked finished paintings at 100-120 °C, rolled them over a cylinder to crack them, and washed India ink into the cracks.
- Supper at Emmaus (1937) was hailed by the top Dutch expert, Abraham Bredius, as "the masterpiece of Johannes Vermeer". It was bought for 520,000 guilders for the Boijmans museum in Rotterdam.
- Göring traded 137 looted paintings for "Christ with the Adulteress" (1942). After the war it was found in an Austrian salt mine.
- He was arrested on 29 May 1945 for aiding the enemy (selling Dutch cultural property) and threatened with the death penalty. He confessed: "The painting in Göring's hands is not a Vermeer, but a Van Meegeren! I painted the picture!"
- Nobody believed him, so between July and December 1945 he painted "Jesus among the Doctors" in front of reporters and court-appointed witnesses.
- The Coremans commission (1946-47) found Bakelite in the paint (a 20th-century material; Bakelite was invented in 1907) and India ink in the cracks.
- In an October 1947 Dutch poll he ranked second most popular person in the country, behind the Prime Minister. On 12 November 1947 he was convicted only of forgery and fraud (one year). He died on 30 December 1947.
- Script simplifications: "in court, he goes 'give me an oven'" compresses the witnessed painting and the commission's tests into one beat. He made the request in custody, and the baking is shown as how he made the fakes. The names (Van Meegeren, Vermeer, Göring) are left out on purpose: the hook formula keeps the subject anonymous, and no real person is drawn as a likeness.
