TOPIC
1. THE ONE-SENTENCE TEST, before any scripting: "[Someone] did [an absurd act], and got [the opposite of what anyone would expect] + [a specific number or scale]." Pass: "He beat the brick tax by making every brick twice as tall." "The French paid an entire city to kill rats, and it ended up with way more rats than before." The act sounds stupid on its own and a real reason makes it clever (or the reverse); that gap keeps people watching.

3. SCRIPT AND VOICE
THE HOOK (sentence 1)
1. ONE sentence of 9-20 words (Rick's median is 12; our best hook, Rat Tails, is 19), past tense, stated as plain fact: a finished absurd act or a rule that contradicts common sense, AND its opposite outcome with a scale. The act lands in the first 2 s, the outcome by about 4.5 s. One concrete object (a bell, a gap, a button, a suitcase), with the absurd part last. Anonymous subject (He / A man / They / A knight / There's a...); a group such as "The French" or "A town" is fine; no personal names or years. Never a scene description, a question or "Did you know". Winners: "The French paid an entire city to kill rats, and it ended up with way more rats than before." / "He beat the brick tax by making every brick twice as tall." Flops gave only the act or a soft result: "A dentist made appointments longer and his patients started bringing him cake." / "They put a deadly plague in one room of a video game and it escaped." Tense: the hook is a finished act in the past (16 of Rick's 20 story hooks: "He poured cooking oil into the sea..."; the 44.2M hit: "He flooded his own basement the day before the river could."); the story then runs in the present ("You see, two companies are racing...").
2. Rick's hooks, for the pattern only: "He saw gaps into a railroad every 39 ft." (9 words; "You see," at 3 s; 14M). "They buried a man alive with a bell tied to his finger." (12; 11M). "He put a bronze ball in a dragon's mouth and told the emperor, 'If this ever falls out, run.'" (19; 31M). "It's impossible to get fired in Japan, but companies found something way worse." (16M). "A man snuck into a family's house every night and moved every single thing one inch to the left."
3. HOOK WORKSHOP (every video, print it): write 6 candidates and score each 0-1 on: 9-20 words; a finished absurd act or contradiction; the opposite outcome with a number or scale word (twice, way more, 10,000); one concrete object; the absurd part at the end; no name or year; a stranger would ask "wait, why?". Keep a 7/7. Read the hook plus the next sentence aloud at 3.8 words per second: the hook ends by about 4.5 s and "You see," starts by 6 s.

"YOU SEE," AND THE BRIDGE
4. OPTION A, when the hook needs a rule of the world explained (9 of Rick's 20 stories; the 15.9M dartboard): sentence 2 starts with "You see," 2.5-6 s in, with no pause after the hook, in present tense ("You see, two companies ARE racing..."). It explains the rule of the world that makes the hook possible, with the setting (time, place) folded in. No sentence of plain scene-setting before it.
5. OPTION B, when the hook explains itself (10 of Rick's 20 stories; the 44.2M basement at 55%, the 6.2M jar at 63%): "You see," comes late, at 55-85% of the runtime, and opens the reveal. Two of Rick's stories and the 15.9M dartboard use both. Use A and B about equally (BH4). A bridge of "Imagine you're...", "But how? Well, picture..." or "Wait, X? Well,..." fits either.

THE BEATS (default formula; the topic decides exact lengths; board AQ shows Railroads and Storm Oil beat by beat)
6. 0-4.5 s the hook. 4.5-6 s "You see," + the rule of the world, said in the home base. 6-20 s the plan or the problem as things the hero DOES there, with the doubter in place by 10 s and one mocking line in quotes. EARLY ESCALATION YOU CAN SEE: the first escalation lands at 13-18 s and the biggest count before 20 s, as countable things piling up in the shot (Rat Tails: 1,000 tails a day, then 10,000, then 20,000, shown as piles; Rick's driver crashes a second car, then brings a buddy, then buys a nicer car just to crash it). 15-35 s one spoken question that deepens the mystery ("Wait, practiced?"). Then escalating beats with time markers (the next week / by Friday / one rainy morning), each a new action by the same people. A fake-out at 30-45% of the runtime: the plan seems to fail and the doubters look right. THE REVEAL IS AN EVENT ON SCREEN (the camera taped to the windshield, the real gold vein behind the blast, the perfect rail kicking sideways, the palace in rubble), with the why told over it or after it in a place we already know (the Jar's pendulum inside the jar; the Japan Job's law, told right after the twist); never a lecture section in a new place. Then the "why" as a CUTAWAY of that same object (the house sliced open with the people inside, the inside of the jar; 8.6, BH2), with at most two quick everyday comparisons (3.14), and ONE "this is real" line after it ("Flood guides really say to let the water in", "Look at any dartboard today", Rick's "sailors really carried oil just for this"). When the story itself really happened, that line says so ("This really happened in Peru."); the shoes video, a true story caught on security cameras, never said it. THE PEOPLE THE HOOK IS ABOUT DISCOVER THE TWIST THEMSELVES, on screen: never a side character who exists to deliver it (the shoes video sent the baker next door to buy stolen shoes at the hideout so he could open the boxes; the thieves should open them, in front of their first customer). The last 3-6 s: the twist or callback, ending on its key word. Rick's 20 story scripts (vidIQ transcripts, BH4) follow this shape with no institution statistic, no "That's the trick" and no debate line; none names its people, and 1 of all 33 says a year.

CRAFT (every line)
7. The hero never explains himself: only cryptic one-liners ("Trust the oil.", "Attack and find out."). The viewer learns the reason at the reveal, together with the doubters.
8. Mockery escalates: one person laughs, then a group, then the whole town or room; the main doubter laughs on screen. NO PERSONAL NAMES for invented people: call them by role and look (his buddy, the rich guy in a white suit, a quiet guy in an old car, his neighbour in the red shirt); none of Rick's 20 story scripts names its people (only his two fictions name a boy, and "slow Eddie" is a nickname), and every name is one more word the viewer has to learn. The doubters give the hero a mocking nickname, and it returns at the payoff (Rick's pattern: "the toy guy", "the clickety clack line"; ours: Butt Picasso, Mr. Two Cars).
9. 1-2 short in-character quoted lines of mockery or doubt, each under 10 words ("You're wrecking my railroad!", "See, pointless.").
10. 1-2 exaggeration jokes ("That horse lost a race to the milk cart, and it was pulling the milk cart.").
11. Odd, specific, SMALL numbers you could count or picture (39 ft, 47 scratches, 3:00 a.m., three short rings, a fifth neck injury, his last hundred bucks). NO YEARS and NO STATISTICS from institutions (police counts, economists' totals, "2.3 million drivers"): 1 of Rick's 33 scripts says a year (an explainer) and none quotes an institution's statistic, and every such number in the Cars video needed its own insert set (a newspaper, a counter sign, a man with a clipboard on a bridge). When scale matters, show it (a whole street of smoking junk cars) instead of saying a figure. Plant one specific detail early that returns changed at the end (47 scratches become 48; POP. 300 becomes 10,000). The story's key number appears once in the world, painted or printed on an object at the moment it matters (section 8.9).
12. Rule of three with the third beat breaking the pattern ("Then one night a ball dropped. Then another. Then all eight at once.").
13. One concrete picture instead of an adjective ("not even a ripple in the emperor's wine"). Every sentence has one concrete thing we can picture, and every sentence raises a question, escalates or pays off; delete any that does none of these.
14. EVERYDAY COMPARISONS: at most two per video, inside the reveal, each one or two short sentences, "Ever [everyday action]?" or "like a [everyday thing]". Rick: "Ever blown on a bowl of soup?" (Storm Oil, 17M), the hairpin turn taken at 60 (Roller Coaster, 13.4M), "think about the last time your phone slid off a table" (Second Draw, 10M), the 19-floor elevator (Random Numbers, 9.7M). The 44.2M basement: "like a parked truck. Ever squeezed an empty soda can?" The 15.9M dartboard: "like a vending machine with a chocolate button between two raisin buttons." Each is SHOWN literally for 1.5-3 s with one moving thing (8.8; BH2). Quick similes ("kicks sideways like a snake") are free and need no picture of their own. Never a comparison that needs its own explanation. A second-person story ("Imagine your boss walks you past your desk...") is a whole-video choice, at most 1 video in 4, and the "you" then follows a specific avatar on screen (section 7.17).
15. No debate line in the script; the debate question goes in the description (2.10). A good last line starts the argument by itself (3.16).
16. The last sentence is the twist or callback: short, quotable, arguable, ending on its key word (the shoes video ended on "...holds a single left shoe up to the camera and waves."; end on the twist: "...and waves at the camera with the left ones."), written so it runs into the hook when the Short loops. Irony, never a moral, a summary or "and that's why". (Japan Job's "because nobody can do nothing forever" drew a 73,847-like top comment.)
17. Casual spoken words ("goes", "pretty sure", "a guy", "Pal"); no sentence over 25 words.
18. SENTENCES FLOW, THEY DO NOT CHOP: average 10-14 words per sentence (Rick 9.7-13.9) and at most about 1 sentence in 8 of 4 words or fewer (Rick 7-28%, median 11%). Join fragments with "and", "so", "but", "because", "while". One punchy fragment run per video at most, at the climax. The kit's three voice rows check this on the final file.

LENGTH AND PACE
19. Multi-beat stories: 62-66 s, about 225-240 words (most of our winners: 226-234 words, 63-65 s; all 19 of Rick's story Shorts since Oct 19 run 57-67 s, median 64 s; his one over 70 s got 0.6M). One-trick stories: 47-50 s, about 180-190 words, at most 1 video in 5 (Rat Tails won at 47 s because nothing was cut from the escalation). Never under 47 s or over 67 s unless I ask for another length. Length comes from the escalating beats and the fake-out, never from extra setup.
20. PACE IS MEASURED IN SYLLABLES: 4.6-4.9 syllables per second on the final voice file (Rick 4.6-4.9 syllables; 3.34-4.07 words per second, median 3.72, across 33 transcripts). Measure it with python3 kit/measure_like_rick.py <render>.mp4 --words words.json (the Voice speed row). Too slow is as wrong as too fast (our videos have measured 4.06, 4.15, 4.25, 4.43 and 5.3; the Skull and Cars voices stayed slow because the take was never measured and fixed at step 2). No long gaps, but never squeeze the voice to fit: if the script is too long, cut words. Do not slow down before "You see,".

VOICE (every video)
21. "The Pharaoh 3" (Qziuou6kCJ2R3w53L2Zs) on eleven_v4, default settings, the whole script in ONE take (no stitched clips).
22. Trim every pause longer than 0.10 s down to about 0.10 s.
23. Then exactly ONE Rubber Band R3 pass for the speed only, at the natural pitch: ffmpeg rubberband=tempo=T:pitch=1.0:formant=preserved:channels=536870912 (build_voice_r3.py T 0). Measure the trimmed take first, then choose T (1.00-1.10) so the final file lands at 4.6-4.9 syllables per second; the measurement decides, not the T value (T 1.12-1.18 once pushed a voice to 5.3, too fast). If the video would then run past 66 s, cut words. If it is still under 4.6 at T 1.10, cut slow filler words and re-voice. channels=536870912 selects the R3 "finer" engine (the default R2 engine sounds robotic). No pitch shift, never atempo plus a second pass.
24. Make words.json (word timings) from the final processed file, and diff its words against the script before rendering (the aligner once wrote "salesman" for "salesmen").
25. Rick's narrator is not any public ElevenLabs voice (checked against 329 library voices); do not search for it and never clone it from his videos.


4. SCENES AND SHOTS
SCENES (boards AQ; archive AA, AB, G, H)
1. A scene = one place at one time. Plan 9-13 scenes for 60-65 s (7-10 for 47-50 s) set in only 3-6 PLACES (sets): most scenes are returns to a place we already know. Rick: Mine Salting uses 6 places and the tunnel carries 36 of 62 s; Instinct uses 5 and the roadside tree and its road carry 40 of 60 s; the Cars video used 19 set-ups and stayed at most 5.5 s in any of them (board BG4). Most scenes last 3-7 s. A scene under 3 s is only for a time jump (that night, the next morning) or a single punch image (a beam, a number, a jaw snapping); never three scenes under 3 s in a row; never a montage of places.
2. HOME BASE: one or two places the story keeps returning to (where the hero and the doubters are), carrying at least 40% of the runtime together, each return again 5-8 s (Rick: the betting room 4 times in Mirror Race; the deck carries most of Storm Oil; the tunnel carries 36 of Mine Salting's 62 s). Build the home base first and best: it gets the most real assets, extras and ambient motion.
3. A scene of 9-17 s falls inside the first 20 s and carries the hook, "You see," or the setup in one place (Jar 0-13.5 s; Railroads 0-11.7 s as one camera flight from tunnel to station). A 3-6 s opening flight or hook shot before it is fine. 2-3 scenes of 9 s or more in total.
4. The reveal and any comparison happen in the home base, the payoff place or inside the story object (the inside of the jar), never in a place built only for them. The climax or payoff spectacle runs 5-12 s (Storm Oil 12.1 s, Railroads 7.2 s, Jar 6.5 s).
5. Inside a long scene the angle may change (close-up, two-shot, reverse) or the scene may be one unbroken camera move; the place, the light and the people stay and everyone keeps acting. A list in the script (several runners, several steps) is shown in ONE scene: the examples are already there, acting one after another in one wider shot, or passed in one slow track with each item centred for at least 1.2 s.
6. ONE NEW ACTION EVERY 1-1.5 s, INSIDE THE SHOT (boards BC2, archive AY1e): in every second of the video at least one of these happens on screen: a character does something with a prop or another character (grab, pour, tuck, carry, dive, point, wipe, drink), a body reacts (flinch, duck, hop, shake, slump), or the story object changes (it fills, cracks, glows, flips, rises). Mouths, blinks, breathing and camera drift do not count. A held pose lasts at most 1.5 s, and only on a punchline reaction. The new action happens INSIDE the shot; the camera does not jump to a new subject on every phrase (that looks frantic). Rick's actions are simple and a little silly, and they make a made-up story look like something that happened: the cook blows on a bowl of soup, the cashier dives head-first into the bin, a desk sign counts days since the last neck injury, the clerk hands over cookies, then chocolate, then shampoo, one item per spoken word. Write one such action per line of the script in the shot list. One clear new event per phrase looks alive; busy, jittery extras look cheap.

SHOTS
7. SHOT COUNT AND SCREEN TIME (boards archive AU, AZ1): 18-28 shot changes per 60-65 s (about 5 per 10 s, counting whips, fast moves and cuts; an average shot of 2.3-3.5 s; Rick's median shot is 3.0 s). A shot usually lasts 2-5 s; a punch insert 1-1.5 s; a mechanism insert 1-2.5 s. A shot of a character or object lasts at least 1.5 s. A shot under 1 s is allowed only inside a chain that follows ONE moving thing from shot to shot (the poured oil down the hull into the sea; the thrown hook landing on the rail), at most 3 in a row, ending on a hold of at least 1.5 s. A character's first appearance lasts at least 1 s inside such a chain and normally 2 s or more; a character who appears once is on screen at least 1.5 s or is cut. Never a sub-1 s shot of a still subject, a stranger, a bare floor, a wall or a blank frame. The hook image, the mockery moment, the fake-out and the explanation each get one shot of 4-8 s in which the subject keeps acting.
8. SHOT MIX (archive AP), as a share of all frames: close-ups about 15-20%, mediums 25-35%, wides 15-20%, object or place with no character 30-40% (Rick 17 / 27 / 19 / 38).
9. SIZE BY JOB (head width as a % of the frame WIDTH):
   - a quoted line, a reaction or an expression beat: CLOSE-UP, head 30-45% (favour a medium close-up for quoted lines);
   - two characters interacting: MEDIUM TWO-SHOT, heads 18-33%, overlapping at different depths and facing each other (section 7.5);
   - a body action with a prop: MEDIUM or FULL, head 18-25%, the prop at least as big as the head;
   - a group (mockery, crowd): heads 8-22%, staggered, the front member biggest;
   - a main character whose head is under 14% of the frame width makes the shot a WIDE: never on a line, a reaction or a quoted word about them;
   - a place, a journey, scale or danger approaching: WIDE, heads under 14% or no character, about 1 shot in 5, 3 s or less each;
   - a mechanism insert: the object fills 40-100% of the frame width, 1-2.5 s;
   - the payoff spectacle: the title object 25-60% of the frame width, clear and readable, 5-12 s, with a reaction close-up just before or after.
   Probably: everyday second-person stories use more interiors and close-ups and fewer wides than historical disaster stories.
10. NO-CHARACTER SHOTS: every dialogue, mockery, doubt and reaction beat has a character in frame. No-character shots are only (1) the opening place, 6 s or less as one moving camera flight, (2) mechanism inserts, and (3) the payoff spectacle of the title object, 5-12 s. Each shows ONE clear subject, big, readable and moving or changing; never smoke, clutter or rows of identical effects covering it, and never an empty frame (empty sky, room, grass, road, plain wall), not even for 0.3 s on a scene change.
11. VARIETY (archive Z1): no two shots in a row with the same subject size and angle. At least 6 shot types per video from: over-the-shoulder, low angle, high angle, profile two-shot, extreme close-up of a prop or face, wide with a foreground framing piece, from behind a character, tracking alongside a walk, top-down, cutaway or diorama, through a window or frame.
12. LAND ON THE NAME: when a line names someone or something, it is on screen while the word is said, never small at the frame edge or hidden behind another character. A move, whip or cut that brings it into frame ENDS on the first syllable of its name, so it starts 6-12 frames before the word.
13. THE PICTURE SENTENCE: every line is shown as WHO does WHAT to WHICH OBJECT, and all three are on screen at the verb's word: the doer whole and readable, the verb happening (not about to happen, not over), the object at 25-60% of the frame width. Write it in the shot list for every line. A size, weight or count claim gets a reference object of known size in the same frame, ideally in one character's hands (a normal brick next to a tall one). Rick does this on almost every line: the cook gripping the oil pot and pouring it on the sea; the loop shape filling the frame; the winners as a wall of photos.
14. Once or twice per video, a scale gag: something at a funny scale (people crawling on a giant map, a tiny model house next to the real one).

CHARACTER CONTINUITY
15. No character vanishes on a scene change. The main characters travel with the camera into the next place (walk, ride, get carried), or the next scene opens on them already there; if the story leaves them, show them leaving (walk off, door shuts, car drives away). The hook's characters are back on screen within 12 s. CAST: 1-2 leads carry the story and are on screen at least half the runtime (Rick: Mine Salting's leads, the salter and his buddy and then the rich guy, about 65%; the Cars video's Memo about 33%); 4-5 people in all plus extras (6.12); everyone who speaks or is named in a line appears in at least two scenes. Never a stranger brought in to hold one prop or say one line (the Cars video's newspaper reader, man in pink, straw-hat neighbour and economist each appeared once; BG4).
16. Within one set, every character in the story at that moment exists on every frame and keeps their position and state across shot boundaries: keep one persistent per-set cast list (positions and states as functions of the global frame) that every shot in the set reads. A new shot reframes the camera; it never removes and re-adds people.
17. Actions flow into each other: each action ends in the pose the next one starts from, with every in-between beat played (running, stumbling, falling, lying; standing, sitting). Actions overlap: one character starts reacting while the other is still finishing. A scene change starts on a motion that continues into the next scene in the same direction and speed.
18. Lasting changes stay visible for the rest of the scene (soot, wet clothes, dents, a broken plank). Objects never pop in or vanish: thrown things fly on an arc, falling things bounce and settle, lifted things swing on their rope.


5. CAMERA AND TRANSITIONS
CAMERA (core AE; archive AJ, Z1, AF, AS, AT, AU, AW)
1. HOW MUCH THE CAMERA MOVES (every frame of Rick's 27 story Shorts; the kit holds the numbers; the middle half of his videos in brackets):
   - Almost never dead still: still on about 9% of frames (5-12%), each still stretch about 0.15 s (90% under 0.4 s). Under every held framing there is a slow drift or push-in.
   - Slow drift (0.3-2 px per frame at 720 px wide) on about 26% of frames (23-34%); clearly moving on about 64% (54-72%). Median speed about 3.3 px per frame (2.2-4.1); fast moves (90th percentile) about 20 px per frame (14-25), never over 25.
   - About 7 distinct moves per 10 s (5.6-7.5); a move lasts about 0.5 s (0.4-0.65), reaches peak speed in about 5 frames (4-7) and eases out. About 70% of moves (64-76%) are mostly push-in or pull-out; the rest are glides, tracks and cranes.
   - The camera is already moving in the first 3 s (about 6 px per frame).
2. CALM STRETCHES (archive AU): Rick changes framing in bursts and then lets a shot run: about 5 shot changes per 10 s (4.0-6.1), the longest gaps between them about 4.5 s (90th percentile, 3.5-5.6), and about 5 calm stretches of 3 s or more per video (4-7; never fewer than 3 in all 27). Plan at least 5 calm stretches of 3-5 s in which the camera only drifts, pushes in or glides slowly while the characters act inside the shot (the opening scene, the mockery, the trick explanation, the comparison, the payoff). Mark them in the shot list with start and end times; the previz must measure at least 5.
3. MOVES ARE SMOOTH AND TRAVEL: eased in and out (smootherstep, never linear), following a character or the action. At least half the shots contain a travelling move (glide, track, crane, orbit, aerial, push past a foreground object) so the video never plays like a slideshow. At least one move per scene carries the camera from one character or object to another that is already there, landing on the next subject on its word (Storm Oil 5.8-11.3 s: one slow 5 s push-in from far away to a two-shot, no cut; Railroads 43.6-46.1 s: from the prop up to the two characters behind it). One continuous move may carry 2-3 lines; a scene longer than 4 s is never one push-in on one person. A move inside a scene is a glide of about 18-40 frames (typically 23), never a 3-frame snap. Relocate by pushing or pulling along the same axis rather than swinging round; a jump of more than about 15 m is a cut or a transition.
4. A held framing is never frozen: it creeps with a slow push-in of 0.12-0.35% per frame (0.6% on a punch word); on holds over 4 s keep the creep at 0.12% per frame or less so the push doesn't crop people at the edges.
5. CAMERA HEIGHT AND DISTANCE, SUBJECT FIRST (archive AS, AT):
   - CHARACTER SHOTS (most of the video): the lens at the character's chest-to-eye height, or up to 1 head above looking slightly down, 1.5-4 m away, so the character fills the frame by its job (4.9).
   - LOW SHOTS are close: the lens is low only when the subject is within about 1-3 m and fills at least 40% of the frame height, or when the lens looks DOWN at ground detail with the character leaning in from above. The ground in front is sharp, lit 3D detail filling at most the lower third. A low lens with the subject more than about 4 m away is banned (the toy-figure view).
   - HIGH OR AERIAL shots look down on the place, the scale and the payoff, with characters small in a big detailed world (3-6 per video); the horizon in the top fifth or out of frame.
   - The horizon never cuts the middle third of a character shot.
   - Lens about 50-65 degrees vertical field of view for character shots; 65-75 only for low close shots and big places. Check every key frame with proj.py and print lens height, subject distance, head width and horizon height next to each shot in the shot list.
6. THE HORIZON STAYS LEVEL: roll at most 0.4 degrees per frame (95th percentile; Rick 0.31-0.67) and level in every hold. Keep camera.up fixed at (0,1,0); no Dutch angles, tilted top-downs, roll on whips, orbits or cranes, hand-held float or whole-frame sway. Impacts get a short shake that decays over about 7 frames (the only roll allowed).
7. The climax and the payoff are shot with the camera moving the whole time (Railroads 58.4-64.7 s: a low glide along the rail, an aerial over the bridge, a head-on push at the train).
8. NOTHING CROSSES THE LENS: no post, pole, tree, sign, barrel or character passes between the camera and the subject, in a move or a hold. Foreground framing pieces (a cat, a hull, a lamp, a soft plant) stay in the outer 20% of the frame and cover at most 15% of it; they may be soft. Film straight across a fence from a raised camera so the rails sit below every face. Over-the-shoulder: the foreground person reads as a person (hat brim, ear, collar, the back of the head), covers at most 25% of the frame and never shows a blurred face.
9. THE CAMERA NEVER PASSES THROUGH OR ENDS INSIDE anything: a character, a prop, a wall, a rope line, a tree crown, a pile. No translucent object over the subject. Check camera-to-character distances along every move (tools/fitpy.py) and stills in the middle of long blends and cranes. INSERTS OF THINGS INSIDE A CLUSTER (fruit in a tree, a coin in a pile): the camera stays outside and the insert is built as its own small set (the hero object, 3-6 hero-quality neighbours, everything else hidden near the lens, a soft background). The camera never goes inside a closed box, bin or drawer unless the inside is lit and the object fills the frame (Rick lights the barrel's inside, Cannonballs 27.5 s); otherwise show it from outside (the cashier's legs sticking out of the bin). Leaves are cutouts (alphaTest about 0.5, transparent false, depthWrite true), never alpha-blended layers.
10. FACES IN FRAME: faces sit in the upper-middle (eyes at about 35-45% of the height), above the caption band. A face or head is never sliced by the top or a side edge, except while a character walks in or out and during a 6-12 frame quick move; extreme close-ups may crop the top or one side of the head as Rick does, as long as both eyes, the brows and the mouth are inside. Legs cropped by the bottom edge are fine.
11. FRAMING CLASS: every character on screen is one of three, written per character in the shot list and checked by the projector on every previz frame: (A) a subject: head whole inside the frame, head at least 14% of the frame width, never touching two frame edges; (B) a deliberate over-the-shoulder foreground: the BACK of the head toward the lens with the subject whole behind (core BF1, Flying Thunder 13 s); (C) small in a wide whose subject is a place or an object, and no line is about that character. Anything else is a fail.
12. NO HEAD ON A HEAD: two head centres on screen are at least 1.1 times the bigger head's width apart, unless the story has them touching.
13. No character is cut in half by a side edge unless it is a class B foreground.

TRANSITIONS (core AE; archive P, Q, R, Z2)
14. A shot change is one of: a quick push or whip with motion blur of 6-12 frames (0.2-0.4 s; most of Rick's changes), a camera move that carries into the next framing, a short dissolve of 6-8 frames (item 16), or a straight cut. HARD CUTS ARE RARE: Rick has about 2 per video (1-4.5); at most 4, never two within 1 s, only for punchlines, impacts and a reaction close-up. A WHIP STAYS IN ONE PLACE: it swings between two subjects close to each other and its in-between frames are a blur of the same set; if the centre of any in-between frame shows a wall, roof, vehicle or tree crown nearer than about 3 m that is neither subject (our working limit, not measured on Rick), use item 15 instead. No transition carries more than 0.4 s of blur or empty frame.
15. Pick by situation:
   - object to person: a subject-position match dissolve (the new subject fades in on the same spot and size, then the camera reveals the place);
   - time passes, or a disaster: a dissolve hidden inside smoke, dust, steam or light;
   - the story's object exists: go through it (a mirror, lens, window, map, painting, newspaper photo);
   - two places share a shape: a shape-match dissolve (the soup bowl into the round sea; a toy bridge into the real bridge);
   - something moves from A to B: ride the motion (a car, a boat, a thrown object, a walking character carries the camera into the next place);
   - same place, new angle: a quick push or whip; a punchline, an impact or a reaction close-up: a straight cut;
   - everything else: a camera move that carries the character into the next place.
   At least a third of the place changes are motivated moves (ride, through, shape match), not cuts.
16. DISSOLVES: a plain crossfade only for a time jump in the SAME place, 6-8 frames, with the characters in the same screen positions. NEVER a crossfade or dissolve between two different places, with or without people in them, unless it is a real shape match (item 15); never ghost bodies; never longer than 8 frames; never a white-out, flash, colour wash or light dip; never a flat card or page sliding across the picture as a wipe. Before a push into an object that dissolves, the character walks out of frame so the dissolve carries nobody. The shot after a dissolve opens on a hold of at least 1.2 s.


6. THE WORLD: LIGHT, COLOUR, SETS, EFFECTS, FOCUS (core BG1-BG4, BE1, BE2; archive AK, AR, C-F, S, T, AH)
The look in one line: flat 2D characters living inside a 3D world built from real modelled objects, photo textures and real light (Rick since Nov 1; core BG1).
LIGHT AND COLOUR
1. A REAL TIME OF DAY AND LIGHT YOU CAN FEEL: every PLACE has one, lit that way, and keeps it every time we return; the light changes only when the story moves time or weather (night falls, the storm arrives), so most videos have 1-3 lights. Never swing warm and cold between shots in the same place and day (the Cars video went from +47 to -47 warm-minus-cool within 10 s; BG4). Day: blue sky with lit clouds (deep blue at the top, pale at the horizon), a white sun about 25-45 degrees up that rakes across the ground, throws long soft shadows and puts a warm rim on characters and props (never flat overhead noon light); soft neutral grey-blue shadows lifted by bounce light. Golden hour: a low orange sun, often behind the subject, long shadows and a peach horizon. Night: deep blue with warm pools of lamp and fire light, dark blue windows, cool shadows, lower exposure; a scene the narration places at night LOOKS like night. Warm interiors against cool exteriors. A LIGHT YOU CAN SEE in every scene (sun through a window, a lamp, a lantern, a brazier, string lights, the moon, headlights, a machine's glow), glowing with bloom and lighting the ground and the people near it, with light shafts through haze where it fits.
2. Colour changes in clear blocks that follow the story (warm setup, deep blue night or storm, bright payoff), only for a story reason (the storm arrives, night falls, a lamp is lit at the turn). No purple-pink grade: purple or pink is at most 20% of a colour strip (one sample every 2 s; Rick 11-30%, mostly sunset skies).
3. SHADOW AND COLOUR NUMBERS (core BE2): real dark shadow (1% or more of the frame near black, luminance under 14 of 255) in at least a third of the frames (Rick: 76% of frames in his median Short, never under 18%), but near-black stays under 2% of the frame in the median frame (outlines and captions included). The darkest 2% of pixels sit around 15-25 (Rick about 20). Shadow share (brightness under 50) about 15% of the frame (Rick 14.6%). Dark objects (hats, iron, tyres) are very dark coloured greys, browns or blues with a highlight, never #000; night is deep blue, not black. Frame means between 15 and 245; mean brightness about 117 (107-132), saturation about 0.46 (0.44-0.50), pure white about 2%. No milky haze over near things.

THE 3D WORLD (three.js)
4. BUILD FROM REAL ASSETS (core BG1-BG3). Rick's Shorts since Nov 1 (Railroads onwards) are 3D worlds made of game-quality modelled objects: real-proportion cars with seams and reflections, hundreds of separate grass blades, sculpted rock, a parasol with every rib (BG2, 1:1 crops). The repo was measured on his older painted Shorts, so its default (canvas-painted textures on boxes) gives the painted-box look of every video of ours so far. Build every set from:
   a. PHOTO TEXTURES: CC0 PBR sets from Poly Haven (or ambientCG) at 1k (diffuse, normal, roughness, AO) on MeshStandardMaterial, with the repeat set by real size (about 1 repeat per 2-3 m) and tinted toward the palette: asphalt, paving, plaster, brick, wood, metal, fabric, rock, sand.
   b. REAL MODELS for props and furniture (Poly Haven has lamps, hydrants, bins, crates, barrels, poles with wires, chairs, tables, stools, plants, trees, rocks, tools, machines); at least half the props in every set are real models. Model in code only what no library has (the story object, vehicles, building shells, counters), with bevels, real proportions, the same photo textures and real detail (window recesses, sills, frames, balconies, gutters, cables), never plain boxes.
   c. LIGHT: a Poly Haven HDRI as scene.environment for reflections and bounce (intensity about 0.5), NOT as the visible background; a painted gradient sky dome with lit clouds; the sun as a DirectionalLight with PCF soft shadows whose shadow camera covers the whole shot; a HemisphereLight; ACES filmic tone mapping; ambient occlusion in every crease and contact (crate on deck, foot on gravel); fog in the horizon colour; bloom only on lamps, windows, fire and sparks. The sun stays fixed within a shot; lit side to shadow side about 3.5:1. NO ink or outline pass on the world (characters keep their own outline).
   d. Canvas painting only for signs, labels, posters and screens.
   e. Use fetch_ph.py and the tested scene in Rick_world_starter.zip; its README lists the gotchas (two versions inside one model file, recentring, cloning, the narrow horizontal field of view in 9:16). Check access first; if polyhaven.com is blocked, use ambientcg.com, then the textures, HDRIs and models in the three.js repo via cdn.jsdelivr.net, and say which worked.
   f. FULL SIZE: render the final at device pixel ratio 1.0, not 0.55. kit/sharpness.py on the final mp4 reads a median of 0.64 or more (Rick on YouTube 0.68, lowest 0.59; ours 0.54-0.62; the Cars video 0.54 before upload; BG2). In the BG3 test the world took 3.2 s per frame at dpr 1.0 and 1.6 s at 0.55, so full size costs about 2x on the 3D pass: pay for it by switching the lens pass off on medium and wide shots (6.16) and redrawing the sun's shadow map every 2nd frame, never by going below dpr 0.8. Never sharpen to pass the number.
5. SET SPEC AND DETAIL (core BE1, BG3): at least 4 photo-textured materials (6.4a) and at least half the props real models (6.4b); about 10 or more props of set dressing, including 3-5 readable story props or signs; depth (a window, a door, a street behind); wear (chips, stains, patches, dirt in the corners, colour shifts); complex structures of many thin parts (rigging, scaffolding, railings, bunting, string lights, wheels and spokes) and organic shapes (rocks, sacks, coiled rope). PROPS ONLY THIS PLACE AND TIME WOULD HAVE: Rick's saloon has a board reading "$20.67 per oz GOLD" (the real old price), his store a wall of lottery tickets and a pegboard of fishing lures, his pachinko hall a ball bucket at every stool and a different screen on every machine. FILL THE FRAME: no flat wall, floor or panel bigger than about a third of the frame. Measured: edge pixels (a brightness step of 25 or more) in the top 62% of the frame, median 12% or more on 10 frames (Rick 15.6%); the kit's background-detail row at least 14 (Rick 14.2-19.8).
6. PROPS HAVE SHAPE: bevelled crates of varied sizes with lids, rope and wear; rounded barrels; planks and rivets; varied building shapes. Never a plain cube crate, a flat slab or a wall of identical stacked boxes. Round things are round: wheels, barrels, bins, cups and plates have at least 24 segments.
7. STORY OBJECTS LOOK REAL: the object the story is about gets real-world texture and wear (an old painting has brush texture, varnish, a worn frame and fine cracks; a document has creases, stamps and handwriting; a skull is a bone-coloured skull with sockets, never a grey blob). Ice, glass and water read as what they are: ice is clear with a pale blue tint, frosted edges and sharp highlights and never takes the colour behind it; glass is a light tint with reflections and the things behind it stay readable; never a see-through coloured panel. An invisible process (heat, cold, pressure) is shown ON the object: a colour change, frost creeping, a thermometer column, steam. Never a bloom, glow or flare that washes over the subject or across text. Keys, tickets, coins and labels in an insert are sharp and bigger than any sparkle on them. Every material is solid unless it is glass, water or ice.
8. THE GROUND AND THE LAYERS: the richest part of Rick's frame is the foreground. Gravel is individual lit stones with their own shadows; sleepers have grain and painted marks; planks have gaps, wear and colour variation; cobbles have height and moss; grass has blades; puddles reflect. Model or instance it near the camera, never a flat tiled texture on a plane. Every frame has a foreground (close to the lens: a sign, a rail, rope, the textured ground), the subject, and a hazy background, so moves show strong parallax. The ground and objects near the lens stay SHARP in medium and wide shots; never a blurred band of dirt or grass across the bottom of the frame and never a big soft blob (a rock, a stone) at the lens.
9. DISTANCE FADES: distant things turn paler and toward the sky colour (atmospheric perspective). Fog tinted to the horizon so far houses are clearly lighter, lower in contrast and softer than near ones. At night, low ground haze lit by the lamps.
10. MATERIALS REACT TO LIGHT: glossy paint and wet stone reflect the sky; metal rails and lamp brass catch hot highlights; cloth and wood stay matte.
11. WATER, SKY, TREES, BUILDINGS: water is a moving surface (normal-mapped or displaced) with sky and sun reflections, darker where deep, foam at hulls and piers, wakes behind boats, never one flat colour plane. The sky has layered clouds lit by the sun with shaded undersides and a glow near the sun or moon, never flat white sticker clouds. Trees are many leaf clusters with lit and shaded sides, gaps the sky shows through and bark, with bushes and grass at their feet, never a green ball on a stick. Buildings get roofs with overhang and tiles, window frames, sills, gutters, doors and wear, never plain boxes with window decals.
12. AMBIENT MOTION AND MICRO-DETAIL: every set has at least three subtle, purposeful moving things: foliage swaying; cloth fluttering in the picture plane as a ripple (about 0.7 Hz, tip travel about 0.1 m); machines idling; flickering light; rising steam or smoke; hanging things swinging; liquids bubbling. Micro-details: dust motes in light beams, puddle reflections of neon, a cat by a bicycle, cables and AC units, a figurine in a window. Particles only where the place calls for them (snow outdoors in winter, dust in a sunbeam, sparks from metal); never weather indoors; never particles over a face. EXTRAS: a place that is busy in real life has 2-8 background people doing that place's job (walking past, waiting at a stop, driving by, sweeping, queueing), smaller and lower in contrast, never facing the lens, never moving in sync, and a street has passing traffic (Rick's camp, army and racetrack crowds; the Cars video's streets were empty while the line said 2.3 million drivers). Extras are not cast: no lines, no close-ups.

FIRE, SMOKE AND EFFECTS
13. FIRE IS A LIGHT: animated flames flickering at 8-12 fps in varied sizes, coming out of windows and roofs (never rows of identical flame cards), each with a warm flickering PointLight that lights walls, ground and faces, plus embers and a dark grey smoke column.
14. SMOKE never covers more than half the frame, or any face, for more than 0.5 s, and never hides the title object or the hero. Explosion smoke is grey-brown with debris, with the hero visible in a corner.
15. Fire, smoke, water, splashes and dust are animated, lit 3D effects, never flat stickers, squiggles or a floating bar or shape standing in for a real object. Cartoon symbols attached to characters stay drawn in 2D: sweat drops, steam puffs, blush, sparkles, impact stars, "Zzz", music notes, tears.

FOCUS (depth of field; archive AY1b)
16. Shallow depth of field on close-ups and object inserts and as a soft foreground frame in the corners. Medium and wide shots are deep focus front to back, including the ground near the lens. About a third of screen time has a visibly soft background. Blur (the skill's lens() cInf): insert 14, close-up 13, full 8; medium, wide and establishing shots have the lens pass switched off (which also pays for the full-size render, 6.4f).
17. WHAT IS SHARP MATTERS MORE THAN HOW MUCH IS BLURRED: focus lands on the eyes of whoever the line is about, or on the story object, and the frame centre sits there too. Every shot names its FOCUS TARGET (a scene-graph node) in the shot list; the focus distance stays within about 5% of it, and the target is the sharpest tile on a 4 x 8 sharpness grid (caption band left out). Never a blurred face or head anywhere in the frame, foreground included; never a soft foreground object over the subject.
18. IN EVERY INSERT THE SUBJECT IS THE SHARPEST AND MOST COMPLETE THING IN THE FRAME: whole (not cut by an edge), 40-70% of the frame width, nothing soft in front of it, the focus distance taken from the scene graph on every frame.
19. Never pull focus inside a held shot: focus changes only while the camera is moving or on a cut (follow(f, [[frame, target, glideFrames], ...]) synced to the move). Check stills at every focus target: a target a few metres off leaves the speaker blurred.


7. CHARACTERS (core BF1, BF2, BF3, AX1, AX2; archive A, AL, AO, AV, AV2, V, AY1d)
WHAT THEY ARE, AND WHAT PUTS THEM INSIDE THE WORLD
1. Rick's characters are flat 2D drawings, and so are ours: a flat white head, a flat coloured body with a dark outline, thin limbs (core BF1, captured at near-full resolution). Never rebuild them as 3D models; that moves away from Rick. A flat character reads as a cardboard standee only when it stands front-on, upright, side by side with others, facing the lens with nothing in front of it, and that is how almost every character shot of the Skull video looked (core BF3). Rick's read as people because of the four things in items 2-6, on every character shot.
2. VIEWS (core BF2): draw every main character in front, 3/4 (both sides), profile and back views, plus a turning in-between (for back to front: the face peeking round the side of the head). Each shot uses the view that fits what the character faces and where the camera is: back view when we look over or past them (Rick holds a back view for 4 s while the man faces the window), 3/4 when they face someone or something beside the camera, profile in a side-on two-shot, front only for a line said straight to us. About half the faces on screen are 3/4 (Rick 12 of 27 measured crops). The hero is centred and front-on for at most 30% of the runtime. A view change goes old view -> one drawn in-between -> new view in 2-3 frames on a motivated beat (Rick, Japan Window 36.20-36.27 s). NO TILTED CARDS: a drawing is never turned more than about 20 degrees away from the angle it was drawn for; beyond that, switch views. In 3/4 the eyes, nose and mouth slide well toward the near side (both eyes in the near half of the face), and the hair, hat brim and ears shift with them. The back view has hair or the back of the hat (never a blank white disc), with a low rounded hairline and a white strip that reads as a neck, never a grin.
3. POSES FOR THE ACTION: draw each main character a pose set and use the one the line needs: standing, walking, sitting (stool, bench, crate, stump, the ground, a vehicle seat), crouching and kneeling, lying (face-down, on the back, stretched across something), leaning (on a counter, rail or wall), carrying with both hands, gripping and using a prop (a hammer swing, a pour, a scanner), pointing, hands on hips, arms crossed, shrugging, laughing with the body shaking. Legs bend at the knees and cross when sitting; bodies lean into every action and heads tilt 5-20 degrees on emotion and listening. CONTACT QUOTA: at least 1 character shot in 3 has a contact pose (sits, lies, leans, kneels, crouches, climbs, carries with both hands, rides or drives, pressed against something); Rick: 7 of the 9 frames on core BF1. Every character stands, sits or lies ON something with a soft contact shadow.
4. INSIDE AND BEHIND REAL THINGS: characters stand behind counters, sit in seats and vehicles, lean on railings, are seen through a windscreen or a window, and overlap each other at different depths, with props crossing in front of them (a counter hides a body, a wiper crosses a face, a foreground shoulder frames a two-shot; core BF1). At least 1 character shot in 4 has the character partly behind or inside something (my working target from Rick's frames, not a measured count). A character is hidden by whatever is in front of it.
5. FACING AND EYE-LINES: people face what they are dealing with: the person they talk to, the machine they play, the object in their hands, the event happening. Two characters in a conversation face each other (3/4 or profile, or an over-the-shoulder), never side by side at one depth both facing the lens during a line between them. Only a character delivering a line or a reaction straight to the viewer looks into the lens.
6. THE SCENE'S LIGHT ON THEM: characters are lit by the scene (no glow, no emissive): they take the light's colour (warm lamp, cool night blue, a machine's glow), get a rim from the key light or a window when it is behind them, and go soft when they sit in a blurred foreground corner or far background. Hats and clothes are shaded (a soft gradient, a darker shadow-side band, a highlight) while the head stays nearly flat.

DESIGN (core AX1 heads, faces, hats; AX2 bodies, costumes, limbs, views, poses, light)
7. Heads are flat, bright WHITE (daylight brightness about 245; Rick 243-252) with almost no shading, for every character: never skin-coloured, tan, pink or grey. The scene's light may tint them slightly (cream under a warm lamp, blue at night); a head never turns orange, red or any strong colour. Check head brightness and colour on a still of every scene.
8. Head shape: a round bean, slightly wider than tall (height about 0.94 of the width), slightly tapered with a little asymmetry and tilt; never a perfect circle, a tall egg, boxy, flat-topped, wide-jawed or pointed.
9. Proportions: about 3.3 head-heights tall (head 1 : torso 1.1 : legs about 1.2); a small rounded bean or capsule body about 0.8 of the head width, with lots of costume detail.
10. Arms and legs: thin dark single strokes, about 0.055 of the head width, often bent, for every character (never thick tubes). Ink-dot or small ball hands; hanging hands rest at about 3/4 of the torso height. Hips about 20% of the head width apart with the legs nearly parallel when standing (never splayed). Feet are small ovals, never long planks. When walking, the knees bend forward in a soft curve; a leg never kinks backwards. Every character has both arms and both legs in every frame unless something in front hides them.
11. Outline: each character carries its own dark plum-brown outline (#241726), slightly thicker on the shadow side, with a slight brush wobble. No outline (body or prop) is ever drawn across a face.
12. Face (W = head width): solid black eyes with no highlights, flat-topped bowls about 15% W wide and 8% deep, under a lid bar that overhangs by about 1% for the half-lid; eye gap 26-31% W. Brows ALWAYS visible, about 15% W above the eyes, short, thick, tapered strokes that do most of the acting (a cap never hides them). Mouths are one brush stroke, fat in the middle with pointed ends; the smirk is one-sided; never an even-width wire line. A simple open mouth with teeth; circle eyes only for shock. The gritted grid of teeth is for anger, strain or a fake smile only, never a plain talking face. No coloured ears. Blush only on laughing or embarrassment. A character keeps every design feature through every expression.
13. Hair, beards, moustaches, hats and caps sit ON the head as separate shapes with their own outline and pattern. A cap's band sits about 20% down the head, never on the eyes; the crown puffs above the skull and leans toward the face; flat caps get panel seams from a top button, a darker band, a highlight streak and a dark crescent peak. Hats have an elliptical top, a curved brim drawn in perspective and a highlight streak; bands and stripes wrap round the head; never a flat rectangle hat or a flat triangle. Hair under a hat is a crescent hugging the skull, bigger on the far side in 3/4; hair is rounded tufts that follow the head's curve, never flat angular patches. Black hats are very dark charcoal or coloured felt with a highlight; hat bands use muted colours.
14. Silhouette and colour: each character is recognisable by hat shape plus costume colour, contrasting with the background. The head is the ONLY white thing on a character: bodies wear clearly coloured, shaded clothes, never a white or light-grey body under a white head (a white garment gets a coloured trim and sits against a darker background).
15. Costume: every main character has 2-3 details (patch, badge, name tag, pocket, buttons, tie, lapels, watch chain, scarf, apron, suspenders, camera strap, moustache, hat band), at least one pattern (dots, stripes, plaid), a darker band on the shadow side, and a prop that fits their job (wallet, hammer, lantern, stopwatch, scanner).
16. THE MODEL SHEET: archive AV and AV2 show our hero Joe rebuilt to this spec (front, 3/4, back, walk, eight expressions); new characters change the hat, hair, costume and colours and keep the head, face, limb and proportion spec. Its back-view neck strip and backwards knee still need the fixes in items 2 and 10.
17. "You" scenes: when the narration says "you", the viewer is a specific avatar the camera follows (an orange hoodie with a camera round the neck; a light-blue shirt with a lanyard).

GROUPS, EXTRAS AND STAGING (core BE2; archive AL)
18. Never 3 or more characters side by side at one depth facing the camera. Stagger them front, middle and back (about 1 m sideways and 1.5-2 m in depth), turn them 3/4 toward each other or the action, and give each a verb (point, pull a sleeve, shake a fist, whisper). A 9:16 medium shot fits about three heads side by side; for a fourth person, a group with an object, or someone behind another, use a 62-66 degree lens, drop a person out of frame, or give them their own shot.
19. BACKGROUND LIFE: every set has 3-8 extras already doing the place's own job at different depths (loading crates, rowing, sweeping, playing their machine, queueing, carrying sacks, eating lunch on a girder), looking at their task, so the camera can travel to them. They react on cue (coworkers stop and turn their heads together), never stand in a line facing the camera.
20. Nobody stands on odd spots (smokestacks, roofs) unless the story says so.
21. Hats stay on: a shock "hat pop" arcs up and lands back within 0.5 s, never frozen in mid-air.

NOTHING PASSES THROUGH ANYTHING
22. Characters, their limbs and the props they hold never go through objects (a car body or door, a desk, a wall, a railing, a crate, a tree) or other characters. Every set exports its solids (walls, furniture, vehicles at their animated positions, rails, posts) as boxes; every mark and walk path keeps at least the character's half width (about 0.3 m) of clearance; walks go round obstacles with extra path points; a character by a car keeps 0.15 m from its body even when a door swings open. Characters stand at least 0.6 m apart at the feet, more when they gesture at each other.
23. Sitting in vehicles: the sitting card goes on a SEATS mark inside the cabin; head below the roof, body inside the glass, no limbs through doors or windscreen. Seated adult card: head top 1.13 m x scale above its mark, chin 0.65 m x scale; use scale 0.78 with the mark about 0.49 m up in a 1.46 m-roof car, 0.53 m in a 1.56 m-roof car. Stars and sparkles round a seated head go at about mark + 0.8 m, inside the cabin.
24. A hand that presses, knocks, paints or opens something stops at the surface; held props stay in front of or on the surface. Thrown and flying things travel on arcs that clear every solid and bounce off the floor.


8. SHOW THE STORY ON SCREEN (core BC2, BF3; archive Y, AJ, AI, AH, AM, BD1, AY1a)
1. FRAME 1: no logo, title card, fade-in or black frame. It shows the hook's absurd act being DONE, big, with the subject already moving (the engineer stomps on the car's nose and it folds; the suitcase of cash opening), not a character standing beside the object. The doer is BIG and close (body at least 40% of the frame height, or head 25% or more of the width) and the body acts with anticipation and follow-through. The act keeps going while the hook is spoken; it never stops after half a second to leave a character standing. It is in the story's real light: a 3 a.m. break-in is a dark shop with torch beams and three men sweeping armfuls of sneakers into boxes, never one grinning man in front of a full, brightly lit wall (the shoes video's frame 1; core BI1).
2. THE TITLE THING is on screen at least 4 times, exaggerated so a child gets it in half a second, filling at least 40% of the frame in the hook and at the reveal, and gets one clear 5-12 s shot. When the story is about a difference, show it next to a normal version (a tall brick next to a normal one). It is a real object in the world, never a floating bar or shape.
3. The title thing CHANGES on screen, and that change is the picture: the process the story depends on happens physically in 3-6 s (the rail turns yellow as the calendar goes from MAY to JULY; the water rises to the load line as cargo goes on, then past it; the coaster loop is bent, hammered wide, then squeezed tall).
4. SHOW EVERY BIG MOMENT, AND THE PICTURE MATCHES THE LINE: every impact, failure, transformation or payoff the narration names happens in frame. The cause arrives, contact lands on the word, then the effect (a jolt, dust, crumpling metal, a decaying shake, the occupant thrown forward). Never cut away just before the contact or open on the aftermath. When the story allows, the action and the danger share one frame (Rick's sawing worker keeps sawing while the train's light grows behind him). The noun in each line is on screen while it is said; the picture never shows something else, a reused earlier shot, or the opposite of the line, and every number on screen matches the line (archive BD1: a board flipping from 5 UP to 8 on "cuts it to eight"). Money reads as money (banknotes, coins, a strongbox). Each joke gets its own small, full set.
5. THE PAYOFF is the biggest, brightest, most detailed moment, held 5-12 s with a strong light change, particles that fit the event (sparks, spray, dust, flying cash) and the camera moving, with one quick reaction close-up before or after.
6. EXPLAIN WITH REAL OBJECTS: by changing the object itself, or with a CUTAWAY of the story object in its own place (the house sliced in half with the people still inside and the soil pushing on the wall; the inside of the jar, zoomed in until the mechanism is big and has faces; core BH2), an in-world model or a top-down view that characters handle. A chalkboard, sign or poster diagram is at most a 1-2 s extra after the real objects have shown it, never the explanation itself.
7. THE MECHANISM AS ONE LABELLED OBJECT per video: the hidden rule becomes a physical thing with a label (a fire alarm locked with a padlock engraved "LABOR LAW"; a breaker panel whose switches read "HOPE", "WORK", "FRIENDS", flicked off one by one; a caliper on the rail gap with a coin in May and only a sheet of paper in July).
8. THE EVERYDAY COMPARISONS (3.14), at most two, each 1.5-3 s, with real 3D objects: best acted by the story's own characters in the story's place (the cook blows on a bowl of soup and the oily spots stay smooth) or pushed into the cutaway itself (the 44.2M basement leans a parked truck on the same sliced wall); a quick neutral close-up with one hand doing the everyday thing is also fine (the same video's hand crushing a soda can on a workbench; ZooBow's vending-machine buttons; core BH2). Never a new set with new people, never over 3 s, never a floating diagram, never an object floating alone (the shoes video's glove hung in front of a dark wall for 2.8 s with no car, no hand and no movement: put it on a car seat and let a hand find the one glove). The demo object sits at 25-55% of the frame height, above the caption, and a hand makes every change. A sliced fruit is a real 3D fruit cut on a board, never a flat painted disc.
9. NO FLOATING WORDS OR FLAT GRAPHICS (core BF3): information is shown with real objects in the world: a newspaper headline, a poster, a crate stamp, a ledger, a slate, a price board, a calendar, a clipboard, a phone screen, a counter on a wall. Numbers, totals and sound words ("x12", "12 COINS", "SLAP!", "PLINK") never float in the air; the Skull video had 11. At most ONE floating graphic per video, for the mechanism (Storm Oil's white flow lines over the wave); it sits just in front of the focus plane (cardNearFocus), above heads, never across a face. No flat 2D shape stands in for an object (a painted disc for a sliced papaya, a yellow oval with a glow ring). Documents (notebooks, tally sheets, letters) are 3D objects held in a hand or lying on a table, shot close in the world; never a flat page card that pops up in front of a face or fills the screen. A highlight on part of an object is a lit glow on exactly those parts, never a big white wedge.
10. PROPS OBEY GRAVITY AND HANDS: every prop is either gripped by a visible ball hand, resting on a surface, or falling on a planned arc; never hovering. A held prop is held: a hand touches it on every frame, and it never passes through a face or body. Steam, smoke and dust are thin moving wisps, never a flat white card; no flat bar or card floats above a head. A pile of things still reads as those things (books have covers and spines, coins are round, bone pieces look like bone). A prop is not glued to a character for the whole video: characters pick props up, use them and put them down.
11. ENDING: one ending, on the callback or the payoff, with a story character in the last shot (a reaction close-up after the spectacle, or the hero in the corner of it) holding a changed expression and, where there is one, the callback prop. The last action is small and specific (he places the last block, the crown slips) with a held reaction of 1-1.5 s, and the video ends on the last spoken word. A modern tie-in, if any, is one sentence said over the story's own characters, never a new place with new people.
12. THE SAY-SEE TABLE (every video): one row per line: the line; its key nouns and its verb; what is on screen while each is said (the object, its size, who does the verb); PASS or FAIL. A row passes only if every noun the viewer needs and the verb are visible and readable at phone size while they are said, with the verb happening, not about to happen or already over. Quantities read as quantities ("full of sneakers" = sneakers piled to the ceiling; "counts money" = a fat stack fanned out, a thumb flicking it). An exit line shows the exit ("storms out" = him storming through the door). Planned at checkpoint 2, measured from the previz frames at checkpoint 5b, and printed in the delivery message with 0 FAILs. The shoes video's 10 failures are on core BI1.
13. THE TWIST WITH THE SOUND OFF: mute the previz and watch the reveal. A stranger must get the twist in 1 second from the picture alone. Show the difference itself, big, next to the normal version, for 2 s or more: six shoes lined up on the floor with every toe curving the same way; a left foot twisted into a right shoe with the man waddling; two shoes held sole to sole that do not mirror; the single left shoe lifted beside its right twin on the wall. When the twist is sameness or something missing, put the normal version in the same frame. Record PASS or FAIL in checkpoint 5b.
14. DEVICES AND POINTS OF VIEW: when a line names a device (a camera, a sign, a clock, a radio, a phone), the device is on screen while it is named, or the shot IS its view (a security-camera frame: desaturated, grainy, a timestamp on the device's own picture). A character who "waves at the camera" waves into that view, never into our lens. A device that matters early returns for the callback (the same CCTV view at the end, now with the owner waving the left shoe). A word for a vehicle matches the vehicle ("tricycle" = three wheels; the shoes video drew a bicycle).


9. ACTING (core AN, BA, BC3, BF2; archive AN2, B, X, AC, AD, AG, AI)
THE EIGHT FACES (core AN; fig.ts names in brackets; add any that are missing)
1. SHOCK: eyes become white circles about twice normal size with a tiny black dot pupil; brows jump up (or pinch in for alarm); a small "o" or flat mouth; a sweat drop [shock, aghast, yikes]. + gritted teeth = breakdown [breakdown]; + wide toothy grin = manic or oblivious [eureka].
2. MOUSTACHE MOUTH (disapproval, authority): a wide downturned arc with flared or curled ends that reads like a handlebar moustache, half-lidded eyes (a straight upper lid cuts the top of the pupil), one brow slightly raised [tache]. Curled UP = smug or gloating [tacheUp]. For some older men and authority figures it is their signature mouth in every emotion. Some characters wear a real moustache instead.
3. MOCKING LAUGH: closed upward-arc eyes, brows up, mouth wide open with the top teeth, blush; the whole group laughs together and their bodies shake [mock with shake: 1; laugh or guffaw for the biggest roar].
4. SCHEMER: half-lid squint + one raised brow + wide toothy grin [scheme]; or closed arcs + a gritted rectangular grin for a polite, fake smile [fakegrin]; quieter [sly, smug].
5. HERO CALM: half-lidded eyes, flat brows, a tiny flat mouth or tiny "o" [neutral, bored]; eyes calmly closed while being yelled at [calm]; a one-sided smirk when he knows something [smug]; set jaw [determined]. Smug half-lidded faces are at most 1 in 6 of all faces on screen.
6. ANGER: brows in a sharp V, squinted line eyes, gritted teeth drawn as a grid [fury; milder angry]; escalates to rage. Vary the rage look from video to video: a red flush filling from the chin, steam puffs, a shaking body and fists, a hat that pops up, a vein on the temple, a wide-open yelling mouth [mouth "tOBig"]; purple [purple: 0..1] is only one option, used at most once in every 3 videos, never by default.
7. WORRY: brows tilted up in the middle, dot or circle eyes, a wavy squiggly mouth, a sweat drop [worry, nervous, dismay, gulp].
8. HAPPY: closed upward arcs + a small smile [smile_closed, joy, beam, proud]. IMPACT: eyes squeezed shut + bared teeth on a crash or strain [strain].

WHEN EACH FACE IS USED (Rick's measured Shorts log 19-23 face changes each)
9. Every character has a default face that fits the role and leaves it only on a story beat: hero = calm or smirk; doubter or authority = moustache mouth or anger; the crowd = laugh; schemer = schemer grin; victims = worry or shock. Hook: the hero's smirk or calm, or the schemer's grin; a warning said aloud gets SHOCK on the person who sees the danger. Mockery beats: the whole group LAUGHS for 2-3 s while the hero stays CALM in the same shot. Dismissal or fake-out: the doubter's moustache mouth or a thrilled grin next to the hero's calm. The boss discovers the "mistake": ANGER, escalating to rage only when the narration calls for it; someone about to be blamed: WORRY with a sweat drop. The twist lands: SHOCK on the person it hits; the hero stays calm or allows a small smile at the payoff. SHOCK is saved for turning points: 3-8 per video, never on the calm hero. THE CONTRAST IS THE JOKE: in every two-shot of a key beat the two faces are opposites (calm vs panic, calm vs laughing, smirk vs angry). Extras: a sweat drop for worry or shock, blush for laughing or embarrassment, steam for rage, a sparkle for a show-off's wink, tears for fake crying, "Zzz" for sleep.
10. NARRATION DRIVES THE FACE (archive AN2): draw the narration literally ("laughing" = laugh, "screaming" = open shout, "goes pale" = pale, "starts sweating" = sweat). When the narration names a visible reaction, the face changes BECAUSE the words say so, step by step as they are spoken, never before the phrase starts, and is complete by the key word (Rick, Railroads 16.6-18.2 s: each word of the phrase adds one change). Smaller verbs are drawn small: "mutters" is a small sideways mouth with lowered brows.

EVERY MOVE HAS ITS IN-BETWEENS
11. NO POSE SWITCHING (core BA, BC3): characters are animated on the rig, never by swapping whole-body poses. Deliberate actions (handing over, lifting, pointing, picking up) take 10-20 frames, small gestures at least 6; only a sudden reaction (a flinch, a jump, arms thrown up) may take 2-4 frames, still settling over the next frames; the face alone may change in 1-2 frames. FAST IS NOT A SWITCH: Rick's quickest moves still show every in-between frame: the rich man pulls dynamite from his coat and raises it beside his head in 11 frames at 30 per second (Mine Salting 47.62-47.99 s); the engineer's slap swings through 3 frames with a smear streak behind the hand (Cart 25.20-25.33 s); the sheriff's axe flies to his hand, visible on each of 4 frames (Instinct 30.7-31.0 s). A hand-off is reach, grip, carry, release, and the prop moves with the hand on every frame. Never switch pose modes from one frame to the next; play a transition action (trip, stagger, sit-down, collapse). When vidIQ describes animation, check the frames before acting on it (it called all three moves above "snapping between poses").
12. TIMING: ease every move in and out (smootherstep). Overlapping action: the eyes and head lead by 2 frames, the torso moves on the beat, the arms and props follow 1 frame late and finish 3 frames later. Arms swing on arcs (elbow blended in polar coordinates round the shoulder, hand round the elbow), never sliding in a straight line. Gestures (point, lift, wave, shrug, reach, pick up, sit, stand) take 8-14 frames. Impacts and strikes (a hit, a slam, a throw, a jump take-off): a wind-up held about 6 frames, the strike in 1-3 frames with in-betweens or a smear, contact on the word, then a 4-5 frame effect (dust, spark, splash, impact star). Each new beat blends from wherever the previous blend had got to. Loose things (cloth, hair, a scarf, dust) follow through and settle, without overshoot. No squash, stretch or overshoot on bodies.
13. FACE CHANGES: each active character changes face about every 2-3 s, always on a beat about them, and holds each face 1-2 s. A change completes on its beat, 0-3 frames before the word: from 4 frames before the beat (s-4) to 2 after (s+2) the brows glide from the old face to the new; the eyes swap inside a 3-frame blink (half at s-2, shut at s-1, half at s); the mouth shows a half-way in-between shape already in the new mood for s-2 and s-1; the head gives a 2-3 degree take. Opposite moods (pleased <-> upset) take 10 frames through a "realising" face: the brows lift while the old face is still on; a blink at s-7 swaps to open eyes, raised brows and a small mouth; that face holds about 5 frames; a half-lid at s-1 brings in the new face on the beat, with a 4-degree head take. If the previous change was less than 12 frames earlier, skip the realising face and hide the swap in a blink. Every expression change goes through a beat in flow(); never switch expr with a ternary or a per-frame condition. A held extreme face ends with one small change (one eye opens, a brow lifts), eased over 4-6 frames. Blinks: 3-4 frames (half, shut as a line, half), every 0.5-1.5 s at irregular times, never on a fixed timer.
14. ONE FACE PER LINE: while a character speaks a line, the eyes and brows keep one shape (blinks aside) and only the mouth moves with the syllables; the face matches the line's mood, so a complaint is never said with a laughing grin. Speakers nod lightly on stressed words (2-3 degrees, about 8 frames, alternating direction), raise the brows on questions and emphasis, and point or gesture on the key word: every spoken line and every claim gets a hand (point, palms up, count on fingers, tap the map, hold up the object).

BODIES (move like real people)
15. Every verb in the narration is acted with the whole body, not just the face, for at least 0.6 s, starting 0-3 frames before its word: sawing rocks the whole body with every stroke and bobs the head; "gets stuck" = kicking legs, arms pushing, a strained face; "laughs" = the body shakes and the head tips back; "shrugs" = both shoulders up, palms up, brows up; "walks past" = legs walking with planted feet.
16. Every shot of 2 s or more contains at least one whole-body action (reach, lift, lean, walk, throw, point, shrug, sit, stand, flinch, heave on a lever with bent legs, swing a hammer with sparks on each hit, tiptoe, sprint and grab). Nobody stands with arms hanging for more than 2 s unless the deadpan is the joke; the deadpan hero still acts (he writes, counts, pushes a stack forward, pockets winnings, shrugs). A held pose is extreme (squeezed eyes, puffed cheeks, whole-body lean) and ends with one small change; a character holding one pose for more than 3 s may shift weight once (1-2 degrees, eased over 12-18 frames).
17. TURNS (core BF2): the head turns first and the body follows about 2 frames later. A small turn slides the face round the head over 1-4 frames; a bigger turn on a drawn character is a swap of drawn views with one in-between drawing (section 7.2), timed at the half-way point of the head blend, never in the middle of a camera move without a blend.
18. WALKS: ease into the first step and stop with a settle step; 9-12 frames per step, body bob 3-7%, lean 1-7 degrees, the stance foot planted (no sliding), the head turning toward where the character is going before the body.

EVERYONE ELSE
19. The character the line is about acts; everyone else faces the action with a readable face. Listeners turn head and eyes toward the speaker or the object (eased over 6-8 frames), react with a face on the line about them, and are busy with a prop (eat, write, polish, hold the brick; Rick's guard lifts a snack, bites and chews while the other guard talks). In a two-shot longer than 3 s both people gesture and the camera travels a little.
20. LIFE LAYER on every character on screen (sec/97_life.ts in the grape project): small purposeful moves (a glance with a 2-degree head turn, a 3-5 degree head tilt, a 1.5-2.5 degree weight shift), spaced 21-39 frames apart at irregular times, each eased over 10 frames and held, with a settle back to neutral on every other beat; seeded per character so nobody moves in sync; skipped while the character does a scripted change; animals keep their own idles. Never a looping sway, wave or breathing bob. The kit's "animation outside camera move" row reads at least 4 (Rick's median 5.0).
21. Group reactions happen together on the same word: bodies shake, heads tip back, someone points at the hero or slaps the desk, while the hero reacts with a face. A line naming a group ("the neighbours", "the whole village") shows the group doing it. Long holds give background people a real task (cranking, restacking, patting soil).

HANDS DO THE WORK (archive AI)
22. Every person verb (saw, paint, pour, lift, carry, roll, rub, stamp, sign, hammer, grab, point, turn the wheel, blow on the soup, slide the tokens, flick the switches) is done on screen by the character's hands and body with the wind-up, the action and the settle, or in a close insert where the object moves as if held and a hand or sleeve enters the frame. Objects never move by themselves while the person who should move them stands idle. Process verbs (shrink, expand, buckle, crack, rise, melt) are done by the object itself with a visible cause (a heat glow, a thermometer, a flipping calendar). Repeat offenders: the pen or brush tip travels along every line as it appears; a hand pulls every visible door open (check the hinge side); a prop changing state animates at its own size; thrown things start at the hand and arc over the shoulder.

MOUTHS (archive AG for shapes; the numbers here win)

2. Never wait on a render with pgrep -f <script name> (it matches its own command line); wait for a chunk file or a log line. After killing a render batch, kill orphaned Chromium processes (parent PID 1, a gpu-process at 100% CPU).
3. Remotion copies public/ into the temp folder on every bundle: point TEMP/TMP at a roomy folder and clean old bundles.
4. Build the cover as a THUMB branch of the first shot (getInputProps().thumb), so frame 1 and the cover show the same moment.
5. Never put code after a // comment on the same line (floodlight stands were silently commented out that way).
CAMERA AND FRAMING
6. Place people and cameras with the projector (proj.py: world point to screen x, y and depth for a pos/look/fov) before rendering stills, including the push-in: a push of k per frame narrows the frame by k x the shot's frame count, so a face at x 0.75 on the first frame ends up cut.
7. Check every fit() camera position against the room bounds (a Python port of fit() plus an "inside the room" test per set). In 9:16 a wide group or a long object seen broadside forces the camera metres away and through a wall: shoot long objects end-on, stagger people in depth, or change the azimuth. Also project the other characters and big props into each hold.
8. Write glides as K(f0, glideTo(prevShot, f0, nextShot, f1, lift, bulge, yLead, lookLead), 1) so they start exactly where the previous framing (including its creep) ended; a K(f, ..., 1) without that is a one-frame jump. A camera key built from a helper that calls screenSide()/walkPath() for the same shot recurses forever; compute positions with polyAt() inside SHOT_CAMS.
9. Per-frame QA can miss the camera passing through a character in a pan: compute camera-to-character distances along every move (fitpy.py). Call root.updateMatrixWorld(true) at the top of World.qa(), or the raycasts use last frame's matrices and report false hits.
10. The interpenetration check (world_qa_pierce.ts.snippet): 8 horizontal rays of 0.2 m x scale out of each standing card at shin, waist and chest height (2+ hits = pierce); one ray up from each seated card's mark to its head top (1.15 m x scale - 0.12); any two cards closer than 0.45 m = pierce. Run with qa_run.mjs (low res, about 14 min per 1900 frames) and summarise with qa_report.py. After any retime, re-run the per-frame QA on the changed range.
11. stops.py (out/t/qa.jsonl) prints every camera stop and each move's peak px/frame; use it to spot frantic stretches, not as a target.
CHARACTERS, PROPS AND SIGNS
12. A held prop can vanish behind a set piece next to the character or behind the head on a hand-to-mouth pose: check one still on every prop beat and use the arm's Top flag (armRTop / armLTop) so a prop held in front of the face draws over it. Arm offsets are screen-space (x+ = screen right) and are not mirrored by facing. Hand props far from the body get clipped by the character card canvas.
13. A card reaching toward a surface that recedes from the camera (a door, a wall) is hidden wherever the surface is nearer the lens than the card plane: move the mark nearer the lens along the camera ray and scale the card down (CastPlace.scale); solve the hand path with proj.py against the surface's world coordinates.
14. Pose-mode switches (stand to brace, stand to kneel) inside a flow() blend snap in one frame: use the continuous controls (mode "kneel" with kneelK, mode "brace" with braceK, 0 = standing, 1 = full) and keep the mode string the same on both sides of the blend. Add new contact poses (sit, lie, lean, crouch, carry) the same way, as continuous controls, so 7.3 never needs a pose swap.
15. Character cards go on the NOINK layer and keep their own drawn outline; the world gets no ink pass. A card whose drawing changes over time (fade, glow, shine sweep, a view change in progress) must put the changing value, quantised, into its texture cache key, or it freezes on its first drawing.
16. Signs the camera pushes into: lay them out as whole lines above and below a centred focal point, so the push trims whole lines instead of cutting words at the sides. Draw any texture the camera gets close to at 4-5x resolution. Size wall signs to sit fully inside the widest shot of the set, or high enough to leave the frame in low shots; a plate under an object must clear nearby edges or oblique cameras hide its first letters. Painted text needs a clear line of sight: the painter stands at the end of the text, not in front of it.
17. Faults that stills catch before a render: the camera stuck inside a building, free 3D assets in the wrong colour, a chain or prop in front of a face, a push that flies past the character, a prop parked between two characters. Check stills at the start, middle and end of every hold, walk, door opening and vehicle move. Lights on stands go in corners, out of every shot's line of sight to its subject; a glow sprite between the camera and an insert's subject reads as a white blob.
18. Foliage sway needs the same vertex offset in any normal or depth pass (sway.ts, material.userData.sway) or edges slide off the leaves. Check a car's forward axis before adding exhaust behind the rear bumper.
REALISM TOOLS (for section 6; the real-asset recipe of 6.4 was tested on core BG3; these extras probably help but are untested in our pipeline, so check stills against core BG1-BG3 before committing)

## Smooth acting implementation (code for master prompt section 9)
Tested in the potato project; a copy lives in the user's repo BenjaminWolff78/Claude-AI-Help on branch `why-he-wanted-his-potatoes-stolen`, folder `templates/cartoon-3d-short/acting/`: `flow_smooth.ts`, `fig.patch`, `world.patch`, `face_qa.py`. If that repo can't be reached, use the code below. The rules this code implements are in master prompt section 9 (items 11-25).
1. **Figure drawer** (`fig.ts` in our pipeline; the matching face and figure code in the repo template's `toon.ts` / `cast.ts`):
   - Add `brow?: [number, number]; swapLid?: number; mouthX?: string;` to FigOpts.
   - Export `faceOf`.
   - In the figure, call the face with `Math.max(blink, opts.swapLid ?? 0)` as the lid, `(opts.mouth ?? opts.mouthX)` as the mouth, and `opts.brow` as a new last argument `browOv`.
   - In `face()`, use `F = browOv ? { ...F0, brow: browOv } : F0`.
2. **World** (where each cast entry gets `blink: lidAt(...)`): after building `o`, add `if (p.o && p.o.blink === undefined && p.o.swapLid) { o.blink = Math.max(o.blink ?? 0, p.o.swapLid); delete o.swapLid; }`. In QA mode, add `faces: [[who#idx, expr, mouth ?? mouthX, brow, lid, back]]` for every cast entry to the per-frame QA line.
3. **Shot header:** replace `mixO` and `flow` with this code. It needs `lerp`, `clamp01`, `pose`, `P`, `Face`, `FigOpts` and `words.json`. Use `talkNod(f, a, b)` by adding it to a speaker's `headTilt` during their line.
```ts
const HANG = (side: number): [[number, number], [number, number]] => [[side * 30, 45], [side * 33, 96]];
/** reference ease: symmetric smootherstep (eases in and out, no overshoot) */
const glide = (x: number) => { const u = clamp01(x); return u * u * u * (u * (6 * u - 15) + 10); };
const gl = (f: number, a: number, d: number) => glide((f - a) / Math.max(1, d));
const bump = (x: number) => (x <= 0 || x >= 1 ? 0 : Math.sin(Math.PI * x));
/** mood of each face: +1 pleased, -1 upset, 0 neutral */
const VAL: Record<string, number> = {
  grin: 1, laugh: 1, relief: 1, relief_open: 1, smile: 1, smile_closed: 1, lick: 1, smug: 1, eureka: 1, sly: 1, joy: 1, guffaw: 1, beam: 1, proud: 1, pitch: 1,
  stern: -1, nervous: -1, shock: -1, dazed: -1, pained: -1, dismay: -1, sheepish: -1, strain: -1, angry: -1, cold: -1, gulp: -1, wired: -1, dead: -1,
  yikes: -1, aghast: -1, green: -1, dizzy: -1,
};
const OPEN: Record<string, number> = { dash: 0, smirk: 0.05, frown: 0.05, wave: 0.1, smile: 0.2, lick: 0.2, o: 0.4, smileWide: 0.4, content: 0.15, flat: 0,
  grin: 0.55, teeth: 0.5, beam: 0.6, O: 0.9, laugh: 0.9, guffaw: 1, teethWide: 0.6, queasy: 0.1, puff: 0, tongue: 0.4 };
/** an in-between mouth: half-way open, in the mood of the face it is heading to */
const midMouth = (ma: string, mb: string, vb: number) => {
  const o = ((OPEN[ma] ?? 0.2) + (OPEN[mb] ?? 0.2)) / 2;
  return vb > 0 ? (o < 0.25 ? "smile" : o < 0.6 ? "smileWide" : "grin") : vb < 0 ? (o < 0.25 ? "wave" : "o") : o < 0.3 ? "dash" : "o";
};
const BRIDGE_BROW: [number, number] = [-5, -4];
const lerpB = (a: [number, number], b: [number, number], k: number): [number, number] => [lerp(a[0], b[0], k), lerp(a[1], b[1], k)];
type FaceState = { expr?: Face; brow?: [number, number]; swapLid?: number; mouthX?: string; headTake?: number };
/** the face at frame f for a swap from A to B landing on frame s */
const faceSwap = (f: number, A: Face, B: Face, s: number, room = 99): FaceState | null => {
  if (A === B) return null;
  // opposite moods need about 12 f since the last face change for the "realising" in-between; closer beats use a blink swap
  const FA = faceOf(A), FB = faceOf(B), va = VAL[A] ?? 0, vb = VAL[B] ?? 0, flip = va * vb < 0 && room >= 12;
  const lid = (c: number) => (f === c - 1 || f === c + 1 ? 0.5 : f === c ? 1 : 0); // a 3-frame blink centred on c
  if (!flip) {
    if (f < s - 4 || f > s + 2) return null;
    const st: FaceState = { expr: f < s - 1 ? A : B, brow: lerpB(FA.brow, FB.brow, gl(f, s - 4, 6)), swapLid: lid(s - 1), headTake: 2.5 * bump((f - (s - 4)) / 7) };
    if (f === s - 2 || f === s - 1) st.mouthX = va * vb < 0 ? "dash" : midMouth(FA.mouth, FB.mouth, vb);
    return st;
  }
  // opposite moods: A -> blink -> "realising" face (open eyes, raised brows, small mouth) -> half-lid -> B
  if (f < s - 10 || f > s + 2) return null;
  const st: FaceState = { swapLid: Math.max(lid(s - 7), f === s - 1 ? 0.5 : 0), headTake: 4 * bump((f - (s - 10)) / 13) };
  if (f < s - 7) { st.expr = A; st.brow = lerpB(FA.brow, BRIDGE_BROW, gl(f, s - 10, 6)); }
  else if (f < s) { st.expr = "neutral"; st.brow = f < s - 3 ? BRIDGE_BROW : lerpB(BRIDGE_BROW, FB.brow, gl(f, s - 3, 5)); st.mouthX = vb > 0 ? "o" : "dash"; }
  else { st.expr = B; st.brow = lerpB(BRIDGE_BROW, FB.brow, gl(f, s - 3, 5)); }
  return st;
};
/** polar blend of an arm (elbow around the shoulder, hand around the elbow): limbs swing on arcs */
const arcArm = (A: number[][], B: number[][], k: number): [[number, number], [number, number]] => {
  const pol = (x: number, y: number) => [Math.hypot(x, y), Math.atan2(y, x)];
  const ang = (a: number, b: number) => { let d = b - a; while (d > Math.PI) d -= 2 * Math.PI; while (d < -Math.PI) d += 2 * Math.PI; return a + d * k; };
  const [ra, ta] = pol(A[0][0], A[0][1]), [rb, tb] = pol(B[0][0], B[0][1]);
  const [ha, hta] = pol(A[1][0] - A[0][0], A[1][1] - A[0][1]), [hb, htb] = pol(B[1][0] - B[0][0], B[1][1] - B[0][1]);
  const r = lerp(ra, rb, k), t = ang(ta, tb), e: [number, number] = [r * Math.cos(t), r * Math.sin(t)];
  const hr = lerp(ha, hb, k), ht = ang(hta, htb);
  return [e, [e[0] + hr * Math.cos(ht), e[1] + hr * Math.sin(ht)]];
};
const NUMS = /^(shake|feetUp|squash|sweat|tilt|headTilt|headBack|lift|monoclePop|wet|look|propK|propRAng|propLAng|hatLift)$/;
const HEAD = /^(look|headTilt|headBack|hatLift)$/, LIMB = /^(armR|armL|propK|propRAng|propLAng)$/;
/** per-part blend: head leads by 2 f, torso on the beat, arms 1 f late and 3 f longer (overlapping action) */
const mixO = (a: P, b: P, f: number, s = 0, bl = 1): P => {
  const kH = gl(f, s - 2, bl), kT = gl(f, s, bl), kA = gl(f, s + 1, bl + 3);
  if (kA >= 1 && kH >= 1) return { ...b };
  const o: Record<string, unknown> = { ...b };
  const keys = new Set([...Object.keys(a), ...Object.keys(b)]);
  for (const key of keys) {
    const va = (a as Record<string, unknown>)[key], vb = (b as Record<string, unknown>)[key];
    const k = HEAD.test(key) ? kH : LIMB.test(key) ? kA : kT;
    const arm = key === "armR" || key === "armL";
    const side = key === "armR" ? 1 : -1;
    const A = arm ? (va ?? HANG(side)) : va, B = arm ? (vb ?? HANG(side)) : vb;
    if (arm && Array.isArray(A) && Array.isArray(B)) o[key] = k >= 1 ? B : k <= 0 ? A : arcArm(A as number[][], B as number[][], k);
    else if (typeof va === "number" && typeof vb === "number" && key !== "facing") o[key] = lerp(va, vb, k);
    else if (typeof va === "number" && vb === undefined && NUMS.test(key)) o[key] = lerp(va, 0, k);
    else if (va === undefined && typeof vb === "number" && NUMS.test(key)) o[key] = lerp(0, vb, k);
    else if (key === "expr" || key === "mouth") o[key] = vb;
    else if (key === "facing") o[key] = kH < 0.5 ? va : vb; // the head turns first, the body follows
    else o[key] = kA < 0.5 ? va : vb;
  }
  return o as P;
};
/** a pose timeline: beats [frame, pose(, blend)]; bodies blend per part, faces swap through a short transition */
type Beat = [number, P] | [number, P, number];
const flow = (f: number, beats: Beat[], blend = 8): FigOpts => {
  let i = 0;
  for (let j = 0; j < beats.length; j++) if (f >= beats[j][0]) i = j;
  if (f < beats[0][0]) i = 0;
  const cur = beats[i][1];
  // each beat blends from wherever the previous blend had got to (no jump when beats overlap)
  const stateAt = (j: number, g: number): P => (j === 0 ? { ...beats[0][1] } : mixO(stateAt(j - 1, beats[j][0]), beats[j][1], g, beats[j][0], beats[j][2] ?? blend));
  let m: P = stateAt(i, f);
  const nx = beats[i + 1]; // the head leads into the coming beat
  if (nx && f >= nx[0] - 2) m = mixO(m, nx[1], f, nx[0], nx[2] ?? blend);
  // faces: the transition into the next beat starts before it, the one from the last beat ends after it
  const exprAt = (j: number): Face => { for (let q = j; q >= 0; q--) if (beats[q][1].expr) return beats[q][1].expr as Face; return "neutral"; };
  let fs: FaceState | null = null;
  for (const j of [i + 1, i]) if (j > 0 && j < beats.length && !fs) fs = faceSwap(f, exprAt(j - 1), exprAt(j), beats[j][0], beats[j][0] - beats[j - 1][0]);
  const out: P = { ...m, expr: exprAt(i), mouth: cur.mouth };
  if (fs) {
    if (fs.expr) out.expr = fs.expr;
    if (fs.brow) out.brow = fs.brow;
    if (fs.swapLid) out.swapLid = fs.swapLid;
    if (fs.mouthX && !cur.mouth) out.mouthX = fs.mouthX;
    if (fs.headTake) out.headTilt = ((out.headTilt as number) ?? 0) + fs.headTake * ((((out.facing as number) ?? 1) >= 0) ? 1 : -1);
  }
  return pose(null, out);
};
/** speech accents: a small head nod (2-3 deg, 7 f) on each word onset inside [a, b) - add it to headTilt of the speaker */
const WF = (WORDS as { w: string; s: number }[]).map((w) => Math.round(w.s * 30));
const talkNod = (f: number, a: number, b: number, amp = 2.5) => {
  let n = 0;
  WF.forEach((o, i) => { if (o >= a && o < b && /[A-Z!?,.]/.test((WORDS as { w: string }[])[i].w)) n += (i % 2 ? 1 : -1) * amp * bump((f - o + 2) / 8); });
  return n;
};
```
4. **Face QA** (`scripts/face_qa.py <qa.jsonl>`, run on the qa_run output). It flags, per character:
   - MOOD FLIP: pleased on one frame and upset on the next. This must be 0.
   - BARE SWAP: an expression change with no blink on either frame. This must be 0, except for the back of the head.
   - BROW JUMP: more than 2.5 units in one frame. This must be 0.
   The per-frame QA log of the shoes pipeline writes `faces` as a string with 8 fields per character (who, expr, mouth, brow, lid, back, facing, mode) and can list one character twice in a frame: parse it with ast.literal_eval, keep the first 6 fields and the first entry per character before running this script.
```python
"""Face continuity QA (smooth-acting rule). Usage: python3 scripts/face_qa.py qa.jsonl
Reads the per-frame QA lines (faces: [who, expr, mouth, brow, lid, back]) and flags, per character:
  MOOD FLIP  - a pleased face on one frame and an upset face on the next (always a fail)
  BARE SWAP  - the expression changes between two frames with no blink (lid < 0.5 on both)
  BROW JUMP  - a brow moves more than 2.5 units in one frame
Talking mouths are expected to change every frame and are not checked here."""
import json, sys
VAL = {k: 1 for k in "grin laugh relief relief_open smile smile_closed lick smug eureka sly joy guffaw beam proud pitch".split()}
VAL.update({k: -1 for k in "stern nervous shock dazed pained dismay sheepish strain angry cold gulp wired dead yikes aghast green dizzy".split()})
rows = [json.loads(l) for l in open(sys.argv[1]) if l.strip()]
rows.sort(key=lambda r: r["f"])
prev, flips, bare, brow, swaps = {}, [], [], [], 0
for r in rows:
    f = r["f"]
    for who, expr, mouth, br, lid, back in r.get("faces", []):
        p = prev.get(who)
        if p and p[0] == f - 1 and not back and not p[5] and not r.get("cut"):
            _, e0, m0, b0, l0, _ = p
            if e0 != expr:
                swaps += 1
                if VAL.get(e0, 0) * VAL.get(expr, 0) < 0: flips.append((f, who, e0, expr))
                if max(l0, lid) < 0.5: bare.append((f, who, e0, expr))
            if b0 and br and max(abs(b0[0] - br[0]), abs(b0[1] - br[1])) > 2.5: brow.append((f, who, b0, br))
        prev[who] = (f, expr, mouth, br, lid, back)
print(f"FACE QA frames {len(rows)}; expression changes {swaps}")
print(f"MOOD FLIP (fail) {len(flips)}: {flips[:30]}")
print(f"BARE SWAP {len(bare)}: {bare[:30]}")
print(f"BROW JUMP {len(brow)}: {brow[:30]}")
```

## Kept code notes (implementation only; every rule is in the master prompt above)

### Life layer (master prompt 9.20)
Wrap every shot's act function (`sec/97_life.ts` in the grape project, copied into the repo's shot_sections) so every character on screen gets the small purposeful moves of 9.20: a glance (eyes plus a 2-degree head turn), a 3-5 degree head tilt or a 1.5-2.5 degree weight shift; spaced 21-39 frames apart at irregular times, with a settle back to neutral on every other beat; each eased over 10 frames and then held, never looped; seeded per character; skipped while the character is busy with a scripted change (gesture, expression, facing, prop, walk); animals keep their own idles.

### Ambient motion and focus helpers (master prompt 6.12 and 6.16-6.19)
- Foliage sways with a vertex offset; any normal or depth pass applies the same offset (`sway.ts`, `material.userData.sway`), or edges slide off the leaves.
- Cloth flutter must move in the picture plane: sway the tips along the rope and lift them slightly, as a ripple down the line (about 0.7 Hz, tip travel about 0.1 m; `flutterV` in the grape remake's road.ts). Check two stills 8 frames apart.
- Liquids bubble: put the bubbles on the front surface of the container as NOINK sprites, or the opaque liquid hides them.
- Focus: `follow(f, [[frame, target, glideFrames], ...])` synced to the camera move; `lens()` cInf by shot size: insert 14, close-up 13, full 8; medium, wide and establishing shots have the lens pass OFF (master prompt 6.16). Measured on the shoes video (2026-11-24): the LensBlur pass on a medium shot drops kit/sharpness.py from 0.71 to 0.54 at dpr 1.0 (the whole frame softens, not only the background), so a video with the lens pass on every shot fails the 0.64 gate however it is rendered. `cardNearFocus` keeps on-screen cards sharp.

### Motion audit (run on every render)
`scripts/motion_audit.py video.mp4 prefix 3 36 45`
- It removes camera motion (ECC affine), then reports 30-px tiles with texture that stay still for more than 36 frames. The caption band is masked.
- Read the overlay frames, not just the number. Buildings, hills and walls never move in the references either, so idle tiles on architecture are fine. Any idle tile on a character, a prop the line names or foliage is a fail.
- Report the before and after figures with the delivery.
- The audit misses motion smaller than a tile (small bubbles in a jug). Check those by eye in two zoomed stills a few frames apart before calling them idle.
- Realistic target: under 20% of frames with a quarter of the textured picture idle.

### Interpenetration QA (master prompt 7.22-7.24 and 13.9-13.10; print it)
- A per-frame interpenetration check over the whole render range. For every cast card and fx object on screen, test the footprint (feet circle 0.3 m) and the body box against the set's solids and the other cards. Fix every hit, then check the rendered video for one-frame pose jumps at each shot boundary.

## Gotchas
- A held prop can vanish behind a set piece next to the character (a door edge, a shelf) or behind the head on a hand-to-mouth pose. Check one still on every prop beat (sip, lift, stamp) and use the arm's Top flag for hand-to-mouth so the prop draws over the face.
- Remotion bundles copy `public/` into the temp folder every time; point TEMP/TMP at a roomy folder and clean old bundles when done.
- The world gets no ink pass. Character cards still go on the NOINK layer and keep their own drawn outline.
- Every hold needs a slow push-in, or QA reads it as a freeze.
- Effect-card textures are cached by key: any card whose drawing changes over time (fade, glow strength, shine sweep) must put the changing value, quantised, into its key, or it freezes on its first drawing.
- The full render at dpr 1.0 takes about 3 hours for a 63 s video with the once-per-frame render loop (see the shoes-video gotchas) and each background job stops at 2 hours: render in resumable chunks and relaunch until every chunk exists. A chunk moved away after the loop has passed it is only redone on the next run.
- Never wait on a render with `pgrep -f <script name>`: the wait loop matches its own command line and never ends. Wait for a chunk file or a log line instead.
- A camera key built from a helper that calls screenSide()/walkPath() for the same shot recurses forever; compute positions with polyAt() inside SHOT_CAMS.
- Check stills in the middle of long camera blends and cranes, not only at the keys: a crane can pass through a wall or bridge face and leave frames with no character.
- **Learned on the forger video (2026-11-06):**
  - `render_range.mjs` caches the bundle URL in `rtmp/bundle_ok_<name>`. Delete that file after ANY code change, or re-rendered chunks silently use the old code.
  - Check every `fit()` camera position against the room bounds before rendering (a Python port of fit() plus a per-set "inside the room" test). In 9:16 a wide group or a long object seen broadside forces the camera metres away and through a wall: shoot long objects (a cart, a table) end-on, and tighten the group or change the az instead.
  - Also project the other characters and big props into each hold: a cart or worker parked "off to the side" can sit right in front of the lens for a different az.
  - Arm offsets are screen-space (x+ = screen right) and are not mirrored by `facing`. A paper or sign held up in front of the face needs `armRTop` (or `armLTop`), or it draws behind the head.
  - Lip-sync: build per-frame visemes from words.json with a grapheme-to-viseme map spread over the voiced frames, plus mood overrides on key words (smile, frown, wide). Check with a face strip cropped from a cast-sheet render before animating that each quoted line moves on every syllable with 3-5 shapes (open shapes 30-45% of the face width).
  - A crossfade that would have people on both sides becomes a straight cut on a beat or a motivated move; never a light dip, wash, flash or white-out, and never a dissolve between two places unless it is a real shape match (master prompt 5.16). Before a push into an object that dissolves, have the character walk out of frame (an acted exit) so the dissolve carries nobody.
  - Signs the camera pushes into: lay them out as whole lines above and below a centred focal point (photo, number), so the push trims whole lines off the top and bottom instead of cutting words at the sides ("SOLD 1943" cropped to "OLD 1943"). Draw any texture the camera gets close to at 4-5x resolution.
  - Pinned notes and posters: check them in the final framing for occluders between the lens and the wall (open wardrobe doors, the hero's hat). A cropped "EVERY" read as "VERY".
- **Camera moves in practice (learned on the grape-brick video, 2026-11-06):**
  - Measure with `scripts/stops.py out/t/qa.jsonl` after the per-frame QA run (copy it from the grape-brick project). It prints every stop, the median, the share under 1.2 s, each move with its peak px/frame (about 44 px per degree of image motion), and the breaks. Dissolve frames count as breaks, not stops. Use it to spot frantic stretches, not as a target.
  - A calm 30-40 frame glide changes the view by only about 10-13 degrees. Relocate by pushing or pulling along the same axis (wide shot to subject, subject to object), not by swinging around. Any relocation over about 15 m (across a road, to another building) is a motivated transition (master prompt 5.15); hard cuts are at most 4 per video, so most relocations ride a camera move.
  - Write glides as `K(f0, glideTo(prevShot, f0, nextShot, f1, lift, bulge, yLead, lookLead), 1)` so they start exactly where the previous framing (including its creep) ended. A `K(f, ..., 1)` without that is a one-frame jump that QA reads as a hard cut.
  - Never start a move right on a dissolve: open every shot on a slow drift or push-in of at least 1.2 s (never dead still; the camera is never still for more than about 0.4 s).
  - Long holds: keep the creep at 0.0012 or less for holds over 4 s, or the push crops the people at the frame edges.
  - In 9:16, two people standing side by side force fit() to put the camera far away, because the width is the binding limit. Stagger them in depth instead (about 1 m sideways, 1.5-2 m in depth). Check where fit() puts the camera against the set's solids (barn, house, walls) with a quick port of fit() before rendering stills.
  - A group framed from along a fence line puts the posts across the lens. Film straight across the fence from a raised camera, so the rails sit below every face.
  - Clear the camera paths of props that sit between two characters (a stacked table, a doghouse). Stills at the start, middle and end of every hold catch them.

## Gotchas learned on the pig-tails video (2026-11-25)

## Gotchas learned on the 200 right shoes video (2026-11-24)
- RENDER LOOP (the biggest speed fix): Remotion's ThreeCanvas defaults to R3F frameloop "always", so while Remotion seeks, waits and screenshots, the requestAnimationFrame loop redraws the whole scene about 10 times per video frame (measured with a readPixels-synced timer around gl.render: 1.35 s scene + 0.35 s post per draw, ~10 draws per frame = 19 s per frame). Pass frameloop="demand" to ThreeCanvas and in the scene component: on every new frame key (shot id + frame) take a delayRender handle in the render body, call invalidate() in a useEffect on that key, and release the handle in useFrame(..., 2) (runs after the EffectComposer's priority-1 draw). Result at full quality (dpr 1.0, MSAA 4, 4096 shadow map, bloom, grade): about 5-6 s per frame, about 3 hours for a 63 s video instead of 16. Check one rendered frame against a renderStill of the same frame (mean abs diff ~1.6 = same frame). ctx.finish() does NOT sync in Chrome's WebGL; time GL with a 1x1 readPixels before and after.
- SHARPNESS IS THE LENS PASS, NOT THE DPR: the shoes video rendered at dpr 1.0 and still measured 0.541 (kit/sharpness.py) because the LensBlur (depth of field) pass ran on every shot. One medium shot: lens on 0.538, lens off 0.708, at the same dpr 1.0. Switch the lens pass off per shot on mediums and wides (master prompt 6.16) and run sharpness.py on the first finished chunk.
- ONE CACHE PER RENDERER for anything GPU-built (PMREM environment maps, render targets): a dissolve mounts a second ThreeCanvas with its own WebGL context, and a module-level phEnv cache handed the first canvas a PMREM texture built in the second, so after the dissolve the next shot lit with a blank environment (half brightness) until the next chunk started fresh. Key such caches by renderer (WeakMap). Symptom to look for: a brightness jump at a chunk boundary in the kit's per-frame luma.
- Never run two Remotion jobs at once on the 2-CPU machine (a still next to a previz times out at 303 s and crashes the other page: "Page crashed!"); keep concurrency 1. Every bundle() call leaves a 380 MB remotion-webpack-bundle-* folder in /tmp: 116 of them filled the disk to 1.6 GB free. Delete old ones (find /tmp -maxdepth 1 -name 'remotion-webpack-bundle-*' -mmin +5 -type d) after each still/bench run.
- SCREEN DIRECTION: a camera looking +z has screen-right = -x; looking +x, screen-right = +z (right = forward x up). Work facings, sweeps and entrances out from this before staging; several shots were mirrored the first time.
- SITTING ON A BOX OR CRATE: put the sit-mode card on the seat's FRONT edge (about 0.3 m from its centre toward the camera), not at its centre. At the centre the crate hides the hips and the legs show through the crate, so the person reads as standing inside the box. Seat height via FigOpts.sitLeg = seatH / HH - 0.25 (HH = 2 * a * 0.94 * SU). When the person stands up, keep the seat height until the pose mode switches and blend the arms (arcArm over ~6 frames), or the card pops.
- Character card textures clip anything more than about 0.65 m from the card centre: draw long held props (bolt cutters, a broom swung wide) as their own FX cards.
- Each set's root is offset by world.ts (set index * 2000 m along x): world-space work (clipping planes, matrixWorld positions of things carried on a moving vehicle) must use root.worldToLocal / root.position.x, and a carried object's parent must updateMatrixWorld(true) before you read it. Clipping planes also need gl.localClippingEnabled = true.
- Camera glides between two framings: check the whole path against seats and props, not only the end frames (a pull-back from a floor-level shot flew through a plastic chair; fix: yLead so the camera rises first). Large props (a covered car) must stay out of every walk path and follow shot; move them to a corner rather than re-routing the cameras. Glides under ~26 frames that also turn or climb measured 80-111 px/frame: give them 34-44 frames.
- A box face or wall that fills the frame to hide a cut is still a hard cut to the kit, and the user saw it as the camera passing through a wall (24.4-25.5 s): use a ride, a shape match or a real move instead.
- A camera standing among seated characters puts a giant head on the frame edge: put it above their heads (about 2.3 m, looking down) or behind them.
- Positions from a step function (a column index from a running count) teleport the character; box-filter them over ~13 frames.
- Poly Haven: white_plaster_rough_01 renders olive/brown (use painted_plaster_wall with a tint); fabric_pattern_05 at a low repeat on a small prop reads as a grid (a glove looked like a chocolate bar): use 60-70 repeats per metre on hand-sized props.
- Debug: an inputProps camOv {pos, look, fov} in Bridge (world coordinates, add the set offset) gives a layout overview still; cards still face the shot camera.
- The night/indoor renders measure dark and dull in the kit (brightness 95, saturation 0.36): a final light pass on the joined video, curves=all='0/0.03 0.25/0.32 0.5/0.59 0.75/0.815 1/1',eq=saturation=1.5, brought them to 113 and 0.44. Better: light the sets brighter so no pass is needed.

## Gotchas learned on the library late-fees video (2026-11-16)
- Point lights cost every pixel in software GL even at intensity 0. Set `light.visible = false` for every lamp a scene does not use (per-scene ctl masks). 20 lamps made frames about 3x slower. Benchmark only with no stray render or chrome processes left running: `ps aux | grep render_chunks` and kill -9 old ones, because orphaned renders respawn browsers and skew every timing.
- MSAA 4 costs about 40% of frame time. Shadow map size does not matter.
- Two render processes on a 2-core, 8 GB box crash the browser (out of memory). That corrupts chunks (Shader Error VALIDATE_STATUS, 56 KB chunk files). Run one process; at dpr 1.0 keep concurrency 1 (concurrency 2 crashed the page on the shoes video).
- The cloud workspace can restart mid-render: chunks survive, the process does not. Always check `ps` and relaunch; render_chunks skips finished chunks.
- When code changes after some chunks were rendered, re-render every chunk that contains a changed frame. The kit flags the seam between an old and a new chunk as a hard cut.
- A frame-difference motion measure on YouTube frames mixes camera movement with on-screen animation. The kit separates them: Rick has LESS camera movement than our videos (clearly moving 64% of frames vs our 80%; fast moves 20 px/frame vs our 38) but MORE animation outside the camera move (5 vs 3). Add life in the frame (props, extras, particles), not camera orbit or creep.
- glideTo with a big lift between a far look and a near look tips the camera down at the floor mid-move. For direction changes use swing(); route moves around heads (a waypoint key) instead of through them.
- A card inside a solid cab cannot be seen through window glass (the cab box hides it). Put the driver card outside the door (leaning out) and film from that side.
- Seated sit-mode heads are 0.85 m above the feet: at a 0.8 m table, raise seated cards 0.3 m or only the tops of their heads show.
- 118 px text on a 512 px canvas clips ("OLD YO"). Size sign text to about 80% of the canvas width.

## Gotchas learned on the Giant Bricks video (2026-11-15)
- At a device pixel ratio below 1 (the old dpr 0.55 final render; finals are now dpr 1.0), NaN or Inf pixels from the scene render get spread by the bloom mip chain into pale rectangles that last 1-3 frames. Put a NaN guard first in the EffectComposer: a Pass whose shader replaces any NaN/Inf pixel with the mean of its valid neighbours (Bridge.tsx NanGuardPass). Check a still at the final dpr, not only at preview resolution.
- Leaf cards built with a cross product against a fixed up vector go NaN when the normal is parallel to it and punch sky-coloured holes: pick the helper axis (1,0,0) or (0,1,0) by whichever is less parallel.
- A shadow caster on a layer the view camera does not render casts nothing: three.js WebGLShadowMap tests object.layers against the VIEW camera. The depth material copies map, alphaTest and side from the object's own material, so a cut-out caster needs its own double-sided alphaTest material.
- The project's camera maths (frame.py scr, frm.py frameOn) does not match renders exactly once lens and depth-of-field effects are on: check framing on a rendered still, not only with the projector.

## Gotchas learned on the crumple-zone video (2026-11-07)
- A character card reaching toward a surface that recedes from the camera (a door, a wall) is hidden by that surface wherever the surface is nearer the lens than the card plane. Move the mark nearer the lens along the camera ray and scale the card down (CastPlace.scale) so it reads the same size; solve the hand path with tools/proj.py against the surface's world coordinates.
- Pose-mode switches (stand to brace, stand to kneel) inside a flow() blend snap in one frame. Use the continuous controls instead: mode "kneel" with kneelK, mode "brace" with braceK (0 = standing legs, 1 = full brace), and keep the mode string the same on both sides of the blend.
- After killing a render batch, kill orphaned Chromium processes (parent PID 1, a gpu-process at 100% CPU); one doubled every later chunk's render time.
- Diff the caption words (words.json) against the script before rendering: the aligner wrote "salesman" for the spoken "salesmen".
- After any retime, re-run the per-frame QA on the changed range before reading stops.py; stale QA logs hide new short holds. Run stops.py from the app folder.

## Gotchas learned on the fire video (2026-11-08)
